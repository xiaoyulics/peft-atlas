Appendix

Mathematical background

The category theory, geometry and group theory the exposés assume, each notion stated with its hypotheses and shown on a PEFT example.

How to use this appendix

This appendix is for readers who know linear algebra, multivariable calculus, basic probability and the PEFT literature, and who have not studied category theory, Lie groups or real algebraic geometry. It skips what ML readers already use, such as the SVD, rank, orthogonal matrices, eigenvalues, Jacobians, gradients, Adam, LoRA and the transformer block. Each of the 93 entries gives a short definition or statement with its hypotheses, a small example from fine-tuning, the places on the site that use it, and usually a textbook for further reading. Definitions carry an ochre rule, standard facts quoted from the literature a dotted teal one, and the one remark a grey one.

Part A covers categories, slices and base change, Part B monoidal categories, string diagrams and lenses, Part C manifolds, cones and real algebraic geometry, and Part D groups, symmetry and conservation laws. The parts can be read in any order, since each entry links to the entries it depends on at their first use. In the exposés, the first use of many key terms is underlined with dots and links to its entry here, and each entry ends with links back to the results that rely on it.

A few symbols from the exposés recur in the examples. A method \(M=(Q,q_0,\rho)\) is a smooth map \(\rho:Q\to\Theta\) from trainable parameters to weights with \(\rho(q_0)=\theta_0\) (A.8), and \(\lvert M\rvert=\dim Q\) is its parameter budget. The expressive dimension \(d(M)\), the first-order image \(S_1\) and the first-order deficit are defined in C.3; the base point is regular for \(M\) when the deficit is \(0\) and an apex when it is positive. LoRA is \(\rho(B,A)=W_0+sBA\) with \(B\in\mathbb R^{m\times r}\) and \(A\in\mathbb R^{r\times n}\), and its full-rank pointings fall into the strata \(\mathrm T_A\), \(\mathrm T_B\) and \(\mathrm S\) of C.19. Block-scalar rates give each factor one learning rate, \(\eta_B\) for \(B\) and \(\eta_A\) for \(A\) (D.23).

A. Categories without tears

20 entries

Part A collects the category theory the exposés use, from the definition of a category up to base change, and illustrates every notion in one running example, the pointed slice \(\mathsf{Meth}(\Theta,\theta_0)\) of weight-space methods. Entries A.1 to A.8 set up categories, functors and slices, A.9 to A.16 cover universal properties, limits and orders, and A.17 to A.19 cover transport, lifting and base change, with A.20 listing the constructions the site names and leaves unused.

Categories and slices

Definition A.1 (category). A category \(\mathcal C\) has objects and, for any two objects \(X,Y\), a set \(\mathcal C(X,Y)\) of morphisms, or arrows, \(f:X\to Y\). Arrows \(f:X\to Y\) and \(g:Y\to Z\) have a composite \(g\circ f:X\to Z\). Composition is associative, and every object has an identity arrow \(\mathrm{id}_X\) with \(f\circ\mathrm{id}_X=f=\mathrm{id}_Y\circ f\).

The site uses \(\mathbf{Set}\) (sets and functions), \(\mathbf{Diff}\) (smooth manifolds and smooth maps), \(\mathbf{FinVect}\) (finite-dimensional real vector spaces and linear maps, where tensor networks are evaluated) and the pointed categories \(\mathbf{Set}_*\) and \(\mathbf{Diff}_*\) of A.7. In \(\mathsf{Meth}(\Theta,\theta_0)\) the objects are methods \(M=(Q,q_0,\rho)\), and an arrow \(h:M\to N\) is a pointed smooth map \(h:Q_M\to Q_N\) with \(\rho_N\circ h=\rho_M\), read “\(N\) simulates \(M\)”. Simulations compose, since \(\rho_P\circ k\circ h=\rho_N\circ h=\rho_M\) for \(h:M\to N\) and \(k:N\to P\). For instance, \(h(B)=(B,A_0)\) is an arrow from LoRA-FA\((A_0)\) to LoRA pointed at \((0,A_0)\). The simulation arrows of the atlas are arrows of this category, each recorded with its map \(h\).

Used in: Notation, Def I.2, Exposé 0–I, VIII.C, Figure 15. Reading: Leinster, Basic Category Theory (2014), Ch. 1.

Definition A.2 (commutative diagram). A diagram is a family of objects joined by arrows, drawn as a directed graph. It commutes if any two directed paths with the same start and the same end compose to the same arrow. A triangle commutes when one side equals the composite of the other two, and a square commutes when its two routes between opposite corners agree.

A simulation is a commuting triangle over \(\Theta\), whose two paths from \(Q_M\) to \(\Theta\) are

\[Q_M\xrightarrow{\;h\;}Q_N\xrightarrow{\;\rho_N\;}\Theta\qquad\text{and}\qquad Q_M\xrightarrow{\;\rho_M\;}\Theta.\]

For \(M=\mathrm{FA}(A_0)\), \(N\) LoRA pointed at \((0,A_0)\) and \(h(B)=(B,A_0)\), both paths send \(B\) to the weight \(W_0+BA_0\) (scale absorbed into \(B\)), so the triangle commutes. Figure 1 draws, for each method, the triangle of Figure A.1, which commutes only up to a pointing defect when the method starts away from \(\theta_0\) (A.19). Commuting squares, used for merging on extensions, return in A.18.

Used in: Figure 1, Def I.2, Def VI.1, Def V.1, Exposé V. Reading: Leinster (2014), §1.1.

Definition A.3 (isomorphism). An arrow \(f:X\to Y\) is an isomorphism, written \(X\cong Y\), if some \(g:Y\to X\) satisfies \(g\circ f=\mathrm{id}_X\) and \(f\circ g=\mathrm{id}_Y\). The inverse \(g\) is then unique. In \(\mathbf{Diff}\) the isomorphisms are the diffeomorphisms, so a smooth bijection need not be one, as \(t\mapsto t^3\) shows. In \(\mathsf{Meth}\) an isomorphism \(M\cong N\) is a pointed diffeomorphism \(h:Q_M\to Q_N\) with \(\rho_N\circ h=\rho_M\). Its inverse lies over \(\Theta\) automatically, since \(\rho_M\circ h^{-1}=\rho_N\).

The LoRA gauge (D.21) \((B,A)\mapsto(Bg^{-1},gA)\), \(g\in GL_r\), is an isomorphism from LoRA pointed at \((B_0,A_0)\) to LoRA pointed at \((B_0g^{-1},gA_0)\). Other examples are rsLoRA \(\cong\) LoRA by \((B,A)\mapsto(\sqrt r\,B,A)\), and \(\mathrm{LoRA}_r\oplus\mathrm{LoRA}_s\cong\mathrm{LoRA}_{r+s}\) by concatenating factors (B.14). Isomorphic methods have the same image, the same \(S_1\) and the same first-order deficit (C.3, Obs I.6). They can still train differently, because the optimizer acts on the coordinates of \(Q\). For LoRA’s gauge with block-scalar rates (one rate per factor), gradient flow is unchanged exactly when \(g\in O(r)\), at points where \(A\) has full row rank and \(r\lt n\) (Thm III.3; D.23).

Used in: Obs I.6, Obs I.7, Three strata, Thm II.2, Prop VI.4, VIII.C.

Definition A.4 (functor; functorial invariant). A functor \(F:\mathcal C\to\mathcal D\) sends each object \(X\) to an object \(FX\) and each arrow \(f:X\to Y\) to an arrow \(Ff:FX\to FY\), with \(F(g\circ f)=Fg\circ Ff\) and \(F(\mathrm{id}_X)=\mathrm{id}_{FX}\). A functor sends isomorphisms to isomorphisms, so \(FX\) is an isomorphism invariant of \(X\). A forgetful functor discards structure, as \(\mathbf{Diff}_*\to\mathbf{Diff}\) discards the base point.

The image is a functor \(\mathrm{Im}\) from \(\mathsf{Meth}(\Theta,\theta_0)\) to the subsets of \(\Theta\) containing \(\theta_0\), ordered by inclusion (a poset, A.16), because an arrow \(h:M\to N\) gives \(\mathrm{Im}\,M=\rho_N(h(Q_M))\subseteq\mathrm{Im}\,N\). In the same way the chain rule at \(q_0\) makes \(S_1\) a functor to the linear subspaces of \(\Theta\), with \(S_1(M)\subseteq S_1(N)\) along every pointed arrow. Read backwards, functoriality gives the atlas test O1 and the first half of O3. If \(\mathrm{Im}\,M\not\subseteq\mathrm{Im}\,N\) or \(S_1(M)\not\subseteq S_1(N)\), then \(N\) does not simulate \(M\). Base change \(\phi_!\) (A.19) and transport \(T_*\) (A.17) are functors between slices.

Used in: Obs I.4(c), Obs I.6, VIII.C, Prop II.13, Def V.1, Backpropagation as a lens. Reading: Leinster (2014), §1.2.

Definition A.5 (natural transformation; naturality). For functors \(F,G:\mathcal C\to\mathcal D\), a natural transformation \(\alpha:F\Rightarrow G\) is a family of arrows \(\alpha_X:FX\to GX\) with \(Gf\circ\alpha_X=\alpha_Y\circ Ff\) for every \(f:X\to Y\). This naturality square says that applying \(\alpha\) and then moving along \(f\) agrees with moving first and applying \(\alpha\) after. A group \(K\) can be viewed as a category with one object whose arrows are the elements of \(K\). A functor from it to \(\mathbf{Set}\) is then a set with a \(K\)-action, and a natural transformation between two such functors is exactly a \(K\)-equivariant map.

The copy map \(\Delta_X(x)=(x,x)\) is natural, \(\Delta_Y\circ f=(f\times f)\circ\Delta_X\), which is rule R7 of the merge calculus (B.4). Elsewhere on the site, natural for a group of changes of chart means equivariant in this sense (D.18). Task arithmetic is natural for all of \(GL(\Theta)\), and DARE for invertible diagonal matrices at a fixed mask (Prop V.4(c)). Gradient flow on LoRA commutes with the gauge \(g\) exactly when the induced cometrics (D.23) agree, which for block-scalar rates singles out \(O(r)\) (Thm III.3).

Used in: Where the category theory works, Thm III.3, When a change of chart does not matter, Prop V.4, Seven local rules, Thm VI.3. Reading: Leinster (2014), §1.3.

Definition A.6 (slice category). For an object \(X\) of \(\mathcal C\), the slice \(\mathcal C/X\) has as objects the arrows \(a:A\to X\), called objects over \(X\), and as morphisms \((A,a)\to(B,b)\) the arrows \(h:A\to B\) of \(\mathcal C\) with \(b\circ h=a\). Composition and identities are those of \(\mathcal C\). The object \(X\) is the base, and \(a\) is the structure map.

\(\mathbf{Diff}/\Theta\) consists of smooth maps \(\rho:Q\to\Theta\) into weight space without base points, and its morphisms are the weak morphisms of Def I.2, which need not send \(q_0\) to \(q_0'\). A slice always has a terminal object, the identity \((X,\mathrm{id}_X)\), which here is full fine-tuning. Working in a slice is the relative point of view of A.19, in which an object is studied together with its map to a base.

Used in: Notation, Def I.2, Where the category theory works, Rosetta entries 3 and 4, Seven borrowed words. Reading: Mac Lane, Categories for the Working Mathematician (2nd ed., 1998), §II.6.

Definition A.7 (coslice; pointed objects; opposite category). The coslice \(X/\mathcal C\) has as objects the arrows \(x:X\to A\) out of \(X\), and as morphisms \((A,x)\to(B,x')\) the arrows \(h:A\to B\) with \(h\circ x=x'\). For the one-point space \(X=\mathrm{pt}\), an arrow \(\mathrm{pt}\to Q\) is a point \(q_0\), so \(\mathbf{Diff}_*:=\mathrm{pt}/\mathbf{Diff}\) is the category of pointed manifolds \((Q,q_0)\) and pointed maps, those with \(h(q_0)=q_0'\). Likewise \(\mathbf{Set}_*:=\mathrm{pt}/\mathbf{Set}\). The opposite category \(\mathcal C^{\rm op}\) has the same objects and every arrow reversed, and \((\mathcal C/X)^{\rm op}\cong X/\mathcal C^{\rm op}\).

A method sends its initialisation to the checkpoint, \(\rho(q_0)=\theta_0\), so it is an arrow of \(\mathbf{Diff}_*\), and a weak morphism forgets the base points and lives in \(\mathbf{Diff}\). Opposites enter in Remark I.3. A simulation \(h:M\to N\) runs from \(Q_M\) to \(Q_N\). The network with \(Q_M\)-parameters is obtained from the one with \(Q_N\)-parameters by precomposing with \(h\), \(f\circ(\rho_M\times\mathrm{id}_X)=f\circ(\rho_N\times\mathrm{id}_X)\circ(h\times\mathrm{id}_X)\), with \(X\) the input space. Read as a change of parameters for networks, \(h\) therefore points the other way, from the \(Q_N\)-network to the \(Q_M\)-network, and Remark I.3 records this reversal with an opposite category (B.16).

Used in: Def I.2, Remark I.3, Obs I.4, What Para adds.

Definition A.8 (the pointed slice \(\mathsf{Meth}(\Theta,\theta_0)\)). Combining A.6 and A.7, \(\mathsf{Meth}(\Theta,\theta_0):=\mathbf{Diff}_*/(\Theta,\theta_0)\). An object is a pointed smooth map \(\rho:(Q,q_0)\to(\Theta,\theta_0)\), and a morphism \(h:M\to N\) is a pointed smooth map with \(\rho_N\circ h=\rho_M\). Equivalently, an object is a factorisation \(\mathrm{pt}\xrightarrow{\,q_0\,}Q\xrightarrow{\,\rho\,}\Theta\) of the point \(\theta_0:\mathrm{pt}\to\Theta\), and a morphism is a map between the middle terms that is compatible with both ends.

On one box, LoRA is \(\big(\mathbb R^{m\times r}\times\mathbb R^{r\times n},\,(0,A_0),\,(B,A)\mapsto W_0+sBA\big)\), and OFT is \(Q\mapsto W_0\,C(Q)\) on skew matrices (block-diagonal in OFT), pointed at \(0\), with \(C\) the Cayley map. Full fine-tuning is \((\Theta,\theta_0,\mathrm{id})\) and the frozen model is \((\mathrm{pt},*,\theta_0)\). The network \(f\) appears nowhere in the definition, so the category depends only on \((\Theta,\theta_0)\) (Remark I.3). The factorisation picture already shows why the frozen model and full fine-tuning sit at the two ends (A.9).

Used in: Notation, Def I.2, Pretrained models and methods, Remark I.3, Rosetta entries 3 and 4.

Universal properties

Definition A.9 (initial and terminal objects; universal property). An object \(I\) is initial if every object \(X\) receives exactly one arrow \(I\to X\), and \(T\) is terminal if every \(X\) has exactly one arrow \(X\to T\). Each is unique up to a unique isomorphism when it exists. Characterising an object by the arrows into or out of it in this way is called a universal property. In \(\mathbf{Set}\) the empty set is initial and a one-point set is terminal, and in \(\mathbf{Set}_*\) and \(\mathbf{Diff}_*\) the one-point space is both.

In \(\mathsf{Meth}(\Theta,\theta_0)\) the frozen model \((\mathrm{pt},*,\theta_0)\) is initial. The only pointed map \(\mathrm{pt}\to Q\) is \(*\mapsto q_0\), and it lies over \(\Theta\) because \(\rho(q_0)=\theta_0\). Full fine-tuning \((\Theta,\theta_0,\mathrm{id})\) is terminal, since an arrow \(h\) from \(M\) must satisfy \(\mathrm{id}\circ h=\rho_M\). So the unique arrow \(M\to\) Full FT is \(\rho_M\) itself, the merge, and every weight-space method that starts at \(\theta_0\) sits in the commuting triangle of Figure A.1.

Used in: Obs I.4(a), Frozen and full fine-tuning, Figure 1, Rosetta entries 3 and 4, VIII.C (rule A1), What base change leaves for the network.

Definition A.10 (product). A product of \(X\) and \(Y\) is an object \(X\times Y\) with projections \(p_X:X\times Y\to X\) and \(p_Y:X\times Y\to Y\) such that for every object \(T\) and arrows \(u:T\to X\), \(v:T\to Y\) there is exactly one arrow \(\langle u,v\rangle:T\to X\times Y\) with \(p_X\circ\langle u,v\rangle=u\) and \(p_Y\circ\langle u,v\rangle=v\). It is unique up to unique isomorphism, and it need not exist. In a slice \(\mathcal C/Z\), the product of \((X,a)\) and \((Y,b)\) is the pullback of \(a\) and \(b\) in \(\mathcal C\) (A.11).

In \(\mathsf{Meth}\) a product \(M\times N\) has arrows to \(M\) and to \(N\), so both factors simulate it, and every method \(T\) with arrows \(u:T\to M\) and \(v:T\to N\) maps to it by exactly one arrow compatible with \(u\) and \(v\). When it exists, its image is \(\mathrm{Im}\,M\cap\mathrm{Im}\,N\). For the one-sided LoRAs with frozen frames \(B_0\) of full column rank and \(A_0\) of full row rank,

\[\mathrm{FA}(A_0)\times\mathrm{FB}(B_0)\;\cong\;\big(\mathbb R^{r\times r},\,0,\,R\mapsto W_0+B_0RA_0\big),\]

with projections \(R\mapsto B_0R\) and \(R\mapsto RA_0\). In the SVD frames of \(W_0\), when \(\operatorname{rank}W_0\ge r\), this is LoRA-XS (Prop I.8(a)). The sum \(\oplus\) of Obs I.7 is a different operation, whose image is a Minkowski sum (B.14).

Used in: Obs I.4(b), Prop I.8, Products, Prop II.3, VIII.C (rule A4), Reading from the right. Reading: Leinster (2014), §5.1.

Definition A.11 (pullback; fibre product). Given \(f:X\to Z\) and \(g:Y\to Z\), a pullback is an object \(P\) with arrows \(p:P\to X\) and \(q:P\to Y\) such that \(f\circ p=g\circ q\), and such that whenever \(u:T\to X\) and \(v:T\to Y\) satisfy \(f\circ u=g\circ v\), exactly one \(t:T\to P\) has \(p\circ t=u\) and \(q\circ t=v\) (Figure A.2). In \(\mathbf{Set}\) the pullback is the fibre product \(X\times_ZY=\{(x,y):f(x)=g(y)\}\). In \(\mathbf{Diff}\), if this set is an embedded submanifold of \(X\times Y\), it is the pullback, and transversality (C.7) is the standard sufficient condition for that. A pullback in \(\mathbf{Diff}\) can fail to exist.

Products in \(\mathsf{Meth}\) are fibre products \(Q_M\times_\Theta Q_N=\{(q,q'):\rho_M(q)=\rho_N(q')\}\). For \(\mathrm{FA}(A_0)\) and \(\mathrm{FB}(B_0)\), with the ranks of A.10, this set is the linear space \(\{(B_0R,RA_0)\}\), so the product exists. For \(\mathrm{LoRA}_1\) and the frozen model on \(1\times1\) weights the set is the cross \(\{ba=0\}\subset\mathbb R^2\), no manifold structure on it has the universal property, and the product does not exist (A.13). Pulling back along the point \(\theta_0:\mathrm{pt}\to\Theta\) gives the fibre \(\rho^{-1}(\theta_0)\), the underlying set of \(M\times\text{Frozen}\) whenever that product exists.

More: HRA is a meet of images, not a product

Take \(W_0\) injective and \(r\) even with \(2\le r\le n-2\). The fibre product of \(\mathrm{LoRA}_r\) with the rotation orbit \(W_0\cdot SO(n)\) has local dimension \(d(\mathrm{HRA}_r)+r^2=rn+r(r-1)/2\) over generic points of the meet (C.19). This exceeds \(rn=\lvert\mathrm{HRA}_r\rvert\) because \(r\ge2\), so HRA is not the product of the two methods, and Thm II.7 keeps only the meet of their images (A.16).

Used in: Obs I.4(b), Prop I.8, Prop II.3, Thm II.7(d), Cor III.13.

Definition A.12 (limit; creation of limits). A cone over a diagram \(D\) in \(\mathcal C\) is an object \(L\) with an arrow to every object of \(D\), such that each triangle formed with an arrow of \(D\) commutes. A limit of \(D\) is a cone through which every other cone factors by exactly one arrow. This categorical cone is unrelated to the geometric cones of C.10. Terminal objects, products and pullbacks are the limits of the empty diagram, of two objects, and of two arrows with a common target. A functor \(U:\mathcal C\to\mathcal D\) creates limits if, for every diagram \(D\) in \(\mathcal C\) and every limit cone of \(U\circ D\), exactly one cone over \(D\) is sent by \(U\) to that limit cone, and this cone is a limit of \(D\).

The forgetful functor \(\mathbf{Diff}_*\to\mathbf{Diff}\) creates limits (A.14). Concretely, when the underlying manifolds have a pullback, the pullback of pointed manifolds is that pullback, pointed at the pair of base points. This is how Obs I.4(b) computes products of methods as fibre products in \(\mathbf{Diff}\).

Used in: Obs I.4(b). Reading: Mac Lane (1998), Ch. V; Riehl, Category Theory in Context (2016), Ch. 3.

Definition A.13 (adjunction). A functor \(F:\mathcal C\to\mathcal D\) is left adjoint to \(G:\mathcal D\to\mathcal C\), and \(G\) is right adjoint to \(F\), written \(F\dashv G\), if there are bijections \(\mathcal D(FX,Y)\cong\mathcal C(X,GY)\) natural in \(X\) and \(Y\). Many forgetful functors are right adjoints, whose left adjoints add the forgotten structure freely.

The forgetful functor \(\mathbf{Diff}_*\to\mathbf{Diff}\) is right adjoint to \(X\mapsto(X\sqcup\mathrm{pt},\mathrm{pt})\), which adds a disjoint base point, since a pointed map out of \(X\sqcup\mathrm{pt}\) is the same as an arbitrary map out of \(X\) (a manifold may have components of different dimensions). Right adjoints preserve limits (A.14), so a product of methods, if it exists, is also a pullback in \(\mathbf{Diff}\) and can be tested with unpointed maps. Obs I.4(b) uses this to rule out \(\mathrm{LoRA}_1\times\text{Frozen}\) on \(1\times1\) weights. The axis curves \(t\mapsto(t,0)\) and \(t\mapsto(0,t)\) in the cross would lift smoothly through the point over the origin, so the differential of the projection from the product to the parameter plane \(\mathbb R^2\) of \(\mathrm{LoRA}_1\) would have rank 2 there. That projection would then be a submersion near the point (C.4), and its image would contain an open set, which the cross does not.

Used in: Obs I.4(b). Reading: Leinster (2014), Ch. 2 and Ch. 6.

Fact A.14 (two standard facts about limits). Let \(\mathcal C\) be a category.

(a) For every object \(X\) of \(\mathcal C\), the forgetful functor \(X/\mathcal C\to\mathcal C\) creates limits. In particular \(\mathbf{Diff}_*\to\mathbf{Diff}\) does.

(b) A right adjoint preserves every limit that exists in its domain.

More: the idea behind (a) and (b)

(a) The structure arrows \(X\to D_j\) of a diagram in \(X/\mathcal C\) form a cone in \(\mathcal C\), which factors through the limit by exactly one arrow, and that arrow is the structure map of the limit. (b) \(\mathcal C(X,G\lim D)\cong\mathcal D(FX,\lim D)\cong\lim\mathcal D(FX,D_j)\cong\lim\mathcal C(X,GD_j)\), naturally in \(X\).

Obs I.4(b) combines them. Products in \(\mathsf{Meth}\) are pullbacks in \(\mathbf{Diff}_*\), which by (a) exist whenever the underlying pullback exists in \(\mathbf{Diff}\), and by (b) and A.13 only then. The transversality hypothesis of C.7 fails at the base point whenever \(\lvert M\rvert+\lvert N\rvert\lt\dim\Theta\), with \(\lvert M\rvert=\dim Q_M\) (C.3), so the products that exist, such as LoRA-XS, come from other structure (A.11).

Used in: Obs I.4(b), Frozen and full fine-tuning. Reading: Mac Lane (1998), Ch. V; Riehl (2016), Ch. 3 and 4.

Definition A.15 (monomorphism; sub-object). An arrow \(m:A\to B\) is a monomorphism if \(m\circ u=m\circ v\) implies \(u=v\) for all arrows \(u,v:T\to A\). A sub-object of \(B\) is a monomorphism into \(B\), two of them being identified when they differ by an isomorphism over \(B\). Testing with maps out of one- and two-point manifolds shows that the monomorphisms of \(\mathbf{Diff}\), and those of \(\mathsf{Meth}\), are exactly the arrows whose underlying map is injective. Such a map need not be an embedding, as \(t\mapsto t^3\) shows.

Freezing coordinates at their base values gives an injective affine map, hence a sub-object, written \(\hookrightarrow\) in the atlas (rule A2). Examples are \(\mathrm{FA}(A_0)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{LoRA}\) pointed at \((0,A_0)\), by \(B\mapsto(B,A_0)\), and MiCA \(\hookrightarrow\) LoRA pointed at \((B_0,0)\), by \(A\mapsto(B_0,A)\). These maps are embeddings, a stronger property than injectivity. The site writes sub-object and never monomorphism. In Cor III.13, LoRA-FA is a sub-object of LoRA and so a different object, while random-\(A\) LoRA and EVA are the same map \(\rho\) with two different base points in its fibre over \(\theta_0\).

Used in: Def I.2, VIII.C (rule A2), Cor III.13.

Definition A.16 (posets; meets; the expressivity order). A preorder is a category with at most one arrow between any two objects; write \(x\le y\) when there is one. It is a poset if \(x\le y\le x\) forces \(x=y\). In a poset the product of \(x\) and \(y\) is their meet \(x\wedge y\), the greatest lower bound, and for subsets ordered by inclusion the meet is the intersection. Two elements are incomparable if neither \(x\le y\) nor \(y\le x\), and \(x\) is maximal if \(x\le y\) implies \(x=y\). Every category has a poset reflection, in which \(X\le Y\) when some arrow \(X\to Y\) exists and \(X,Y\) are identified when arrows run both ways. A Hasse diagram draws a finite poset with \(y\) above \(x\) when \(x\lt y\), joining them by an edge when nothing lies strictly between.

The poset reflection of \(\mathsf{Meth}\), which we call the expressivity order, orders methods by simulation. Figure 15 draws it for the core methods as a layered Hasse diagram, with mutually simulating families on one rung. A meet of images always exists and need not be the image of a product. For injective \(W_0\) and even \(r\le n-2\), the image of HRA without Gram–Schmidt is the meet of LoRA’s cone with the rotation orbit \(W_0\cdot SO(n)\) (D.11), and HRA is not their product (Thm II.7). Split and zero-init LoRA have incomparable images (Thm II.2(d)), so neither simulates the other. The frontier problem asks for the maximal methods of budget at most \(k\), which are automatically pairwise incomparable.

Base change and lifting

Definition A.17 (automorphism of a category; transport of structure). An automorphism of a category \(\mathcal C\) is a functor \(F:\mathcal C\to\mathcal C\) with an inverse functor, so that both composites are identity functors. It carries initial objects, products, isomorphisms and every other notion defined from arrows alone to notions of the same kind. Transport of structure builds such a functor from an invertible map of the underlying data.

For \(T\in GL(\Theta)\) put \(\tau_T(\theta)=\theta_0+T(\theta-\theta_0)\) and \(T_*M:=(Q,q_0,\tau_T\circ\rho)\). An arrow \(h:M\to N\) is also an arrow \(T_*M\to T_*N\), since \(\tau_T\circ\rho_N\circ h=\tau_T\circ\rho_M\), so \(T_*\) is a functor, with inverse \((T^{-1})_*\). It keeps \(d(M)\) and the first-order deficit (C.3) and the gauge group (D.21), and moves \(S_1\) to \(TS_1\). It does not keep rank, which depends on the matrix shape of \(\Theta\) that \(T\) need not respect. Sums of \(k\) Kronecker products are \(\mathcal R^{-1}_*\mathrm{LoRA}_k\), LoRA on the matrix rearranged by Van Loan and Pitsianis and carried back (B.11), and HiRA is \((D_{W_0})_*\mathrm{LoRA}\) with \(D_{W_0}(X)=W_0\odot X\), when \(W_0\) has no zero entries. The atlas records \(M\) and \(T_*M\) as one object in two frames, with no arrow between them.

Definition A.18 (lift; lifting problem; obstruction). Given arrows \(p:E\to B\) and \(f:A\to B\), a lift of \(f\) along \(p\) is an arrow \(g:A\to E\) with \(p\circ g=f\). A lifting problem asks whether a lift exists, and an obstruction is an invariant whose value shows that none does. A morphism \(h:M\to N\) of \(\mathsf{Meth}\) is exactly a pointed smooth lift of \(\rho_M\) along \(\rho_N\).

In \(\mathbf{Set}\) a lift exists once \(f(A)\subseteq p(E)\), by choosing preimages, so smoothness is the real constraint. For \(\rho_M(t)=t\) and \(\rho_N(u)=u^3\) on \(\mathbb R\) the images agree, and the only lift is the cube root, which is not differentiable at \(0\). A pointed smooth lift forces \(S_1(M)\subseteq S_1(N)\) by the chain rule, so \(S_1\) is a first-order obstruction, and here \(S_1(M)=\mathbb R\not\subseteq S_1(N)=0\). For \(t^3\) against \(u^5\) both \(S_1\) vanish, yet the only lift \(t^{3/5}\) is not \(C^1\), so that obstruction is invisible to \(S_1\). Merging a method on an extension is again a lifting problem, to find \(\mu\) with \(\Phi_f\circ\mu=\Phi_{\tilde f}\circ\rho\), a lift along the realisation \(\Phi_f\) (B.16, Def VI.1).

Definition A.19 (base change; pushforward \(\phi_!\); the relative point of view). A map of bases \(\phi:S'\to S\) induces the pushforward \(\phi_!:\mathcal C/S'\to\mathcal C/S\), \((X,x)\mapsto(X,\phi\circ x)\), by postcomposition. When every object over \(S\) has a pullback along \(\phi\), it also induces the pullback \(\phi^*:\mathcal C/S\to\mathcal C/S'\), \((Y,y)\mapsto(Y\times_SS',\text{projection})\), and then \(\phi_!\dashv\phi^*\). In algebraic geometry, base change usually means \(\phi^*\).

Def V.1(a) uses \(\phi_!\) on pointed slices. A function-preserving pointed reparametrisation \(\phi:(\Theta',\theta_0')\to(\Theta,\theta_0)\), with \(f\circ(\phi\times X)=f'\), gives \(\phi_!:\mathsf{Meth}(\Theta',\theta_0')\to\mathsf{Meth}(\Theta,\theta_0)\), \(\rho\mapsto\phi\circ\rho\), for instance for a permutation of hidden units. Whether \(\phi_!\) carries LoRA to LoRA is the covariance question of Obs II.15. A quantiser changes the function and induces no such functor, so QLoRA and ReLoRA only borrow the name, and Exposé V computes pointing defects and reachable sets for them.

Grothendieck’s relative point of view replaces a space \(X\) by a morphism \(X\to S\), an object over a base. Properties then belong to the morphism, and the useful ones are those that survive pulling back along every change of base \(S'\to S\). The fibre over a point \(s\) is itself the pullback along \(s:\mathrm{pt}\to S\). The site takes this stance in a modest form. A method is a morphism over the base point \(\theta_0\), and quantisation, restarts and merging are read as moves of that base point.

Monoidal categories

Definition B.1 (monoidal and symmetric monoidal categories). A monoidal category is a category \(\mathcal C\) with a functor \(\otimes:\mathcal C\times\mathcal C\to\mathcal C\), a unit object \(I\), and natural isomorphisms \((A\otimes B)\otimes C\cong A\otimes(B\otimes C)\) and \(I\otimes A\cong A\cong A\otimes I\), the coherence isomorphisms, which satisfy the pentagon and triangle equations. Mac Lane’s coherence theorem then makes every diagram built from them commute, so brackets can be dropped. The category is symmetric if it also has natural isomorphisms \(\sigma_{A,B}:A\otimes B\to B\otimes A\) with \(\sigma_{B,A}\sigma_{A,B}=\mathrm{id}\) that are compatible with the associator (the hexagon equation). Examples are \((\mathbf{FinVect},\otimes,\mathbb R)\), \((\mathbf{FinVect},\oplus,0)\) and \((\mathbf{Diff},\times,\mathrm{pt})\).

On methods, \(M\oplus N\) runs the two parameter spaces side by side and adds their updates (B.14). This makes \(\mathsf{Meth}(\Theta,\theta_0)\) symmetric monoidal with the frozen model as unit. Its coherence isomorphisms are those of \(\times\) on pointed manifolds, and they lie over \(\Theta\) because addition is associative and commutative.

Used in: Obs I.7, Sums, Where the category theory works, Thm II.10, Thm VI.3. Reading: Mac Lane (1998), Ch. VII and XI.

Definition B.2 (direct sum and tensor product in FinVect). \(\mathbf{FinVect}\) has finite-dimensional real vector spaces as objects and linear maps as morphisms, and two monoidal products. The direct sum \(V\oplus W\) is a biproduct, both the categorical product and the coproduct, with \(\dim(V\oplus W)=\dim V+\dim W\); on matrices \(f\oplus g\) is block-diagonal. The tensor product \(V\otimes W\) has \(\dim(V\otimes W)=\dim V\cdot\dim W\) and is not a categorical product; on matrices \(f\otimes g\) is the Kronecker product (B.10). Each bilinear map \(V\times W\to U\) factors through a unique linear map \(V\otimes W\to U\).

Two boxes drawn side by side therefore mean a block-diagonal map in the activation diagrams of Exposé VI, whose monoidal product is \(\times=\oplus\), and a Kronecker product in a tensor network. Dimensions multiply along \(\otimes\), which is why the weight of a cut in Thm II.10 is a product of wire dimensions. LoRA’s residual branch \(W_0+BA\) uses \(\oplus\), since it sends one input to two branches and adds their outputs (Figure B.1).

Used in: Thm II.10, Exposé II.C, Thm VI.3, Obs I.7.

Definition B.3 (string diagrams). A string diagram draws a morphism \(f:A\to B\) of a monoidal category as a box with an input wire \(A\) and an output wire \(B\). Composition \(g\circ f\) joins boxes in series, \(f\otimes g\) places them side by side, an identity is a bare wire, the unit \(I\) is drawn as no wire, and in a symmetric category \(\sigma\) is a crossing. Joyal and Street proved that the pictures are sound and complete. In a monoidal category, diagrams related by a planar deformation denote the same morphism; in a symmetric one, diagrams with the same boxes joined in the same way do. Laws such as \((g\otimes g')\circ(f\otimes f')=(g\circ f)\otimes(g'\circ f')\) can then be read off the picture.

The plug of Exposé II.C is a string diagram. LoRA is a box \(A:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^r\) followed by \(B:\mathbb R^r\to\mathbb R^m\), drawn beside \(W_0\) (Figure B.1, read left to right). The merge calculus moves an edit along wires by equalities of maps, so a successful slide proves that the merged network computes the same function.

Used in: Exposé II.C, Thm II.10, Figure 6, Seven local rules, Analogy in Exposé VI, Thm VI.3. Reading: Selinger, “A survey of graphical languages for monoidal categories” (2011).

Definition B.4 (cartesian categories; copy and discard). A monoidal category is cartesian when \(\otimes\) is the categorical product \(\times\) and \(I\) is a terminal object \(1\). Every object then has a copy map \(\Delta_X:X\to X\times X\), \(x\mapsto(x,x)\), and a discard map \({!}_X:X\to1\), and copy is natural, \((f\times f)\circ\Delta_X=\Delta_Y\circ f\) for every \(f:X\to Y\). Conversely, by a theorem of Fox (1976), a symmetric monoidal category is cartesian exactly when every object carries a commutative comonoid, a copy and a discard map obeying coassociativity, counit and commutativity laws (B.6), compatible with \(\otimes\) and natural in all morphisms. \(\mathbf{Set}\), \(\mathbf{Diff}\) and \((\mathbf{FinVect},\oplus)\) are cartesian. \((\mathbf{FinVect},\otimes)\) is not, since \(x\mapsto x\otimes x\) is not linear.

Naturality of copy is rule R7 of the merge calculus. A linear edit \(E\) on a wire read by \(W_Q\), \(W_K\) and \(W_V\) equals \(E\) applied on each of the three branches, so it is absorbed as \(W_QE\), \(W_KE\), \(W_VE\). The site builds \(\mathbf{Para}(\mathcal C)\) (B.16) over a cartesian \(\mathcal C\). The linear copy of B.6 copies basis vectors only and is a different map.

Used in: Def I.1, Seven local rules, Analogy in Exposé VI, Thm VI.3.

Definition B.5 (compact closed categories; cups, caps and wire-bending). A symmetric monoidal category is compact closed if every object \(A\) has a dual \(A^*\) with a cup \(\eta_A:I\to A^*\otimes A\) and a cap \(\varepsilon_A:A\otimes A^*\to I\) satisfying the snake equations \((\varepsilon_A\otimes\mathrm{id}_A)(\mathrm{id}_A\otimes\eta_A)=\mathrm{id}_A\) and \((\mathrm{id}_{A^*}\otimes\varepsilon_A)(\eta_A\otimes\mathrm{id}_{A^*})=\mathrm{id}_{A^*}\), with the unitors \(I\otimes A\cong A\cong A\otimes I\) of B.1 suppressed. Drawn as bent wires, cups and caps turn an input wire into an output wire and back. In \(\mathbf{FinVect}\) the cap is evaluation, the cup is \(1\mapsto\sum_ie_i^*\otimes e_i\), and bending gives \(\mathrm{Hom}(V,U)\cong U\otimes V^*\). With a fixed basis, a box slid around a cup or cap comes out as its transpose.

Bending the input wire of an \(m\times n\) matrix gives its vectorisation in \(\mathbb R^m\otimes\mathbb R^n\), and the Van Loan–Pitsianis rearrangement (B.11) is a composite of bends and crossings. Rule R5 of the merge calculus, \(\langle Mq,k\rangle=\langle q,M^\top k\rangle\), slides \(M\) around the cap that computes an attention score. The cut-rank bound uses only \(\otimes\) and crossings.

Used in: Thm II.11, Thm VI.3, Exposé II.C. Reading: Coecke and Kissinger, Picturing Quantum Processes (2017).

Definition B.6 (Frobenius algebras and spiders). A Frobenius algebra on an object \(V\) is a monoid \(\mu:V\otimes V\to V\), \(\eta:I\to V\) and a comonoid \(\delta:V\to V\otimes V\), \(\varepsilon:V\to I\) obeying the Frobenius law \((\mu\otimes\mathrm{id})(\mathrm{id}\otimes\delta)=\delta\mu=(\mathrm{id}\otimes\mu)(\delta\otimes\mathrm{id})\). A basis \(e_1,\dots,e_n\) of \(\mathbb R^n\) gives one, with copy \(\delta(e_i)=e_i\otimes e_i\), merge (the multiplication; unrelated to merging an adapter into the weights) \(\mu(e_i\otimes e_j)=e_i\) if \(i=j\) and \(0\) otherwise, \(\eta=\sum_ie_i\) and \(\varepsilon(e_i)=1\). It is commutative and special (\(\mu\delta=\mathrm{id}\)). For such algebras the spider theorem says that every connected diagram of \(\mu,\eta,\delta,\varepsilon\) with \(k\ge1\) inputs and \(l\) outputs equals one spider, which sends \(e_i^{\otimes k}\) to \(e_i^{\otimes l}\) and every other basis tensor to \(0\).

Merge multiplies coordinates, \(\mu(x\otimes y)=x\odot y\). So for matrices \(X,Y:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m\) the Hadamard product is the spider composite \(X\odot Y=\mu_m\circ(X\otimes Y)\circ\delta_n\). LoHa is a copy spider on the input feeding \(A_1\) and \(A_2\), then \(B_1\) and \(B_2\), then a merge spider on the output, and in Thm II.10 a spider split by the cut counts its dimension once. The copy and \(\odot\) nodes of Exposé VI live in a cartesian category (B.4), where copy is \(x\mapsto(x,x)\); together they fail the Frobenius law, so there “spider” is only a name.

Used in: Exposé II.C, Thm II.10, Prop II.12, Analogy in Exposé VI. Reading: Coecke, Pavlovic and Vicary (2008).

Tensor networks and rank

Definition B.7 (tensor networks, contraction, bond dimension). A tensor network \(\Gamma\) is a string diagram in \((\mathbf{FinVect},\otimes)\), possibly with spiders. Each box is a tensor and each wire a finite-dimensional space. A wire joining two boxes means contraction, a sum over the index the wire carries. The open wires (legs) are the inputs \(V\) and outputs \(U\), and contracting every internal wire gives the value \([\![\Gamma]\!]:V\to U\). The dimension of an internal wire is its bond dimension. One value has many presentations, and quantities read off a network, such as its cuts, depend on the presentation.

LoRA’s update is the two-box network \(B\circ A\) with one bond of dimension \(r\), and \(\Delta W=BA\) contracts it, \((BA)_{ij}=\sum_{k\le r}B_{ik}A_{kj}\). HiRA factors \(W_0\) through a bond of dimension \(\operatorname{rk}W_0\), while MoRA, C3A and MPO adapters have no narrow cut between inputs and outputs (the table after Thm II.10).

Used in: Thm II.10, Exposé II.C, Figure 6, Where the category theory works, Known results. Reading: Orús, “A practical introduction to tensor networks” (2014).

Fact B.8 (the min-cut bound on rank). Let \(\Gamma\) be a tensor network with input legs \(V\) and output legs \(U\), with every wire attached to a box (put an identity box on any bare strand). Split the boxes into an input side \(\mathcal I\) and an output side \(\mathcal O\). The weight \(w\) of the cut is the product of the dimensions of the wires joining the two sides and of the legs attached on the wrong side (input legs at \(\mathcal O\), output legs at \(\mathcal I\)); a spider with legs on both sides contributes its dimension once. Then \(\operatorname{rank}[\![\Gamma]\!]\le\min_{\text{cuts}}w\). For sums of networks, \(\operatorname{rank}(X+Y)\le\operatorname{rank}X+\operatorname{rank}Y\).
More: the idea of the proof

Contract each side separately. The value becomes a map into the tensor product of the crossing wires and wrong-side legs followed by a map out of it, and that space has dimension \(w\).

For LoRA the cut between \(A\) and \(B\) crosses one wire, so \(\operatorname{rank}BA\le r\) (Figure B.1). For LoHa the cut with \(A_1,A_2\) on the input side crosses two bonds and gives \(r_1r_2\). The bound depends on the presentation. HRA’s product of \(r\) reflections expands into \(2^r-1\) terms of rank at most one, while the telescoping sum \(R-I=\sum_k(H_k-I)H_{k+1}\cdots H_r\) gives \(r\).

Used in: Thm II.10, Exposé II.C, Figure 6, Prop II.12, Known results. Reading: Kolda and Bader, “Tensor decompositions and applications” (2009).

Definition B.9 (tensor trains, Tucker and MPO; gauge on bonds). A tensor train (TT) writes a tensor with legs of dimensions \(n_1,\dots,n_d\) as a chain of cores \(G_k\in\mathbb R^{r_{k-1}\times n_k\times r_k}\), \(r_0=r_d=1\), joined by bonds of dimensions \(r_1,\dots,r_{d-1}\). An MPO is the same chain with one input and one output leg on each core. Tucker joins a core \(C\in\mathbb R^{r_1\times\cdots\times r_d}\) to factor matrices \(U_k\in\mathbb R^{n_k\times r_k}\). Inserting \(g^{-1}g=\mathrm{id}\), \(g\in GL_{r_e}\), on a bond \(e\) and absorbing each factor into its neighbouring box leaves the value unchanged, so the gauge (D.21) contains \(\prod_eGL_{r_e}\). When every \(r_e\) is the rank of the matching unfolding (minimal ranks), the tensors of these ranks form a manifold of dimension \(\sum(\text{core sizes})-\sum_er_e^2\).

LoRA is the two-core case, \(B\) and \(A\) joined by one bond, with gauge \((B,A)\mapsto(Bg^{-1},gA)\) and, for \(r\le\min(m,n)\), image dimension \(r(m+n)-r^2\). TT and Tucker adapters have \(d(M)=\lvert M\rvert-\sum_er_e^2\) at minimal ranks (Prop II.12), and under gradient flow each bond carries a conserved matrix (Isometries and charges).

Used in: Prop II.12, Exposé II.C, Isometries and charges. Reading: Oseledets (2011); Holtz, Rohwedder and Schneider (2012); Koch and Lubich (2010).

Definition B.10 (Kronecker product, vec, tensor product of subspaces). For \(C\in\mathbb R^{m_1\times n_1}\) and \(D\in\mathbb R^{m_2\times n_2}\), the Kronecker product \(C\otimes D\in\mathbb R^{m_1m_2\times n_1n_2}\) is the block matrix \([c_{ij}D]\), the matrix of \(C\otimes D\) in the product basis. It obeys the mixed-product rule \((C\otimes D)(E\otimes F)=CE\otimes DF\), and \(\operatorname{rank}(C\otimes D)=\operatorname{rank}C\cdot\operatorname{rank}D\). The map \(u\otimes v\mapsto uv^\top\) identifies \(\mathbb R^m\otimes\mathbb R^n\) with \(\mathbb R^{m\times n}\). For subspaces \(U\subseteq\mathbb R^m\) and \(V\subseteq\mathbb R^n\), \(U\otimes V\) is then the space of matrices with column space in \(U\) and row space in \(V\), of dimension \(\dim U\cdot\dim V\).

LoKr’s update is \(C\otimes BA\) with \(C\) ranging over all \(m_1\times n_1\) matrices, \(B\in\mathbb R^{m_2\times r'}\) and \(A\in\mathbb R^{r'\times n_2}\). For inner rank \(r'\le\min(m_2,n_2)\) its rank \(\operatorname{rk}C\cdot\operatorname{rk}(BA)\) is at most \(\min(m_1,n_1)\,r'\), and this is attained. So \(\mathrm{Im}\,\mathrm{LoKr}\) lies inside the image \(W_0+\mathcal M_{\le r}\) of zero-init LoRA\(_r\) exactly when \(r\ge\min(m_1,n_1)\,r'\) (Thm II.11(e)). At a LoRA point \((B,A)\), the directions reached by moving one factor alone overlap in \(\{XA\}\cap\{BY\}=\mathrm{col}\,B\otimes\mathrm{row}\,A\), of dimension \(\operatorname{rk}A\cdot\operatorname{rk}B\) (Prop II.3).

Fact B.11 (Van Loan–Pitsianis rearrangement; Kronecker rank). Let \(m=m_1m_2\) and \(n=n_1n_2\), and cut \(\Delta\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}\) into an \(m_1\times n_1\) grid of \(m_2\times n_2\) blocks \(\Delta_{ij}\). The rearrangement \(\mathcal R(\Delta)\in\mathbb R^{m_1n_1\times m_2n_2}\) has the rows \(\mathrm{vec}(\Delta_{ij})^\top\), ordered with \(i\) running fastest. Then \(\mathcal R(C\otimes D)=\mathrm{vec}\,C\,\mathrm{vec}\,D^\top\), and \(\mathcal R\) is a linear bijection that permutes entries, hence a Frobenius isometry. So the Kronecker rank \(\operatorname{rk}_\otimes\Delta\), the least \(k\) with \(\Delta=\sum_{i\le k}C_i\otimes D_i\), equals \(\operatorname{rank}\mathcal R(\Delta)\), and the nearest sum of \(k\) Kronecker products in Frobenius norm comes from the rank-\(k\) truncated SVD of \(\mathcal R(\Delta)\) (Van Loan and Pitsianis, 1993).
More: the idea of the proof

Block \((i,j)\) of \(C\otimes D\) is \(c_{ij}D\), so its row in \(\mathcal R\) is \(c_{ij}\,\mathrm{vec}(D)^\top\). Linearity extends this to sums, and Eckart–Young applies to \(\mathcal R(\Delta)\) because \(\mathcal R\) preserves the Frobenius norm.

In string-diagram terms \(\mathcal R\) bends and crosses wires, regrouping the four legs of \(\Delta\) from \(U_1U_2\mid V_1V_2\) to \(U_1V_1\mid U_2V_2\). A sum of \(k\) Kronecker products is therefore LoRA\(_k\) transported by \(\mathcal R^{-1}\) (Prop II.13), with rank taken across another cut. For \(N=s^2\), \(I_N=I_s\otimes I_s\) has Kronecker rank \(1\) and rank \(N\). Figure 5 animates \(\mathcal R\). On this site a Kronecker sum means a sum of Kronecker products; matrix analysis uses the same name for \(C\otimes I+I\otimes D\).

Used in: Thm II.11, Prop II.13, Figure 5, Prediction X.5, Known results, Key references. Reading: Van Loan, “The ubiquitous Kronecker product” (2000).

Definition B.12 (Schmidt and operator-Schmidt decompositions). Every \(u\in\mathbb R^{m_1}\otimes\mathbb R^{m_2}\) has a Schmidt decomposition \(u=\sum_{i\le\rho}\sigma_ix_i\otimes y_i\) with \(\sigma_i\gt0\) and orthonormal families \((x_i)\), \((y_i)\). It is the SVD of the \(m_1\times m_2\) reshape of \(u\) (\(x\otimes y\mapsto xy^\top\)), and the Schmidt rank \(\rho_u\) is the rank of that reshape, at most \(\min(m_1,m_2)\). The SVD of \(\mathcal R(\Delta)\) likewise gives the operator-Schmidt decomposition \(\Delta=\sum_{k\le K}s_kC_k\otimes D_k\), with \(s_k\gt0\), Frobenius-orthonormal families \((C_k)\) and \((D_k)\), and \(K=\operatorname{rk}_\otimes\Delta\).

If \(u\) and \(v\) have Schmidt ranks \(\rho_u\) and \(\rho_v\), the mixed-product rule gives \(uv^\top=\sum_{i,j}\sigma_i\tau_j\,(x_iz_j^\top)\otimes(y_iw_j^\top)\), an operator-Schmidt decomposition with \(\rho_u\rho_v\) terms, so \(\operatorname{rk}_\otimes(uv^\top)=\rho_u\rho_v\). A generic rank-one update, which LoRA\(_1\) reaches, thus needs \(\kappa_1=\min(m_1,m_2)\min(n_1,n_2)\) Kronecker terms (Thm II.11(c)).

Fact B.13 (rank of a Hadamard product; face-splitting product). For \(X,Y\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}\), \(\operatorname{rank}(X\odot Y)\le\operatorname{rank}X\cdot\operatorname{rank}Y\). Explicitly, if \(X=B_1A_1\) and \(Y=B_2A_2\) with inner dimensions \(r_1\) and \(r_2\), then \[X\odot Y=(B_1\bullet B_2)\,(A_1^\top\bullet A_2^\top)^\top,\] where the face-splitting product \(B_1\bullet B_2\in\mathbb R^{m\times r_1r_2}\) has as row \(i\) the Kronecker product of row \(i\) of \(B_1\) and row \(i\) of \(B_2\). In particular \(W_0\odot(BA)=\sum_{k\le r}\mathrm{diag}(b_k)\,W_0\,\mathrm{diag}(a_k)\), summed over the columns \(b_k\) of \(B\) and the rows \(a_k\) of \(A\), has rank at most \(r\operatorname{rank}W_0\) (Horn and Johnson, 1991, Ch. 5).
More: the idea of the proof

\(X\odot Y\) is the submatrix of \(X\otimes Y\) on the rows \((i,i)\) and the columns \((j,j)\). In pictures it is the spider composite \(\mu\circ(X\otimes Y)\circ\delta\) of B.6, which factors through the two inner wires.

LoHa\(_{r_1,r_2}\) therefore has rank at most \(r_1r_2\), and \(h=(B_1\bullet B_2,(A_1^\top\bullet A_2^\top)^\top)\) is the atlas arrow LoHa\(_{r_1,r_2}\to\) LoRA\(_{r_1r_2}\). HiRA’s update \(W_0\odot BA\) has rank at most \(r\operatorname{rank}W_0\), which allows full rank when \(W_0\) has full rank (Prop II.9(a)).

Sums, Para and lenses

Definition B.14 (sums of methods; Minkowski sums, differences and translates). For subsets \(S,T\) of a vector space, the Minkowski sum is \(S+T=\{s+t:s\in S,\ t\in T\}\), the Minkowski difference is \(S-T=\{s-t:s\in S,\ t\in T\}\), a translate of \(S\) is \(v+S=\{v+s:s\in S\}\), and \(C^{+K}\) is the sum of \(K\) copies of \(C\). A sum is the image of \(S\times T\) under addition, so for semialgebraic sets \(\dim(S+T)\le\dim S+\dim T\) (C.18), and likewise for differences. If \(0\in C\), the additive monoid (D.10) generated by \(C\) is \(\mathrm{Mon}(C)=\bigcup_KC^{+K}\), and if moreover \(\mathbb RC=C\) it is \(\mathrm{span}\,C\). Writing \(\rho_M=\theta_0+\delta_M\), the sum of methods \(M\oplus N=(Q_M\times Q_N,\,(q_0,q_0'),\,\theta_0+\delta_M+\delta_N)\) has image \(\theta_0+(\mathrm{Im}\,\delta_M+\mathrm{Im}\,\delta_N)\), and \(\oplus\) is a symmetric monoidal structure with the frozen model as unit (B.1).

Concatenation \(([B_1\ B_2],[A_1;A_2])\) is an isomorphism \(\mathrm{LoRA}_r\oplus\mathrm{LoRA}_s\cong\mathrm{LoRA}_{r+s}\) of unpointed objects, with the \(A\)-blocks rescaled to the target scale when the scales differ, as under \(\alpha/r\) scaling (Obs I.7). It is also a pointed isomorphism onto \(\mathrm{LoRA}_{r+s}\) pointed at the concatenated initialisation. RoSA and GraLoRA are \(\oplus\)-words. The categorical product of Obs I.4, when it exists, has image \(\mathrm{Im}\,M\cap\mathrm{Im}\,N\) (A.10). Merging and restarting \(K\) times with a fixed shape \(C\) reaches \(\theta_0+C^{+K}\). Since \(\mathcal M_{\le r}^{+K}=\mathcal M_{\le\min(Kr,m,n)}\), ReLoRA reaches every matrix after \(\lceil\min(m,n)/r\rceil\) cycles (Thm V.3).

Re-pointing an additive method \(\rho=\theta_b+\delta\) with shape \(C=\mathrm{Im}\,\delta\) at a parameter \(q\), so that \(\theta_b=\theta_0-\delta(q)\), gives the translate \(\theta_0+(C-\delta(q))\) as image, and all exact pointings together reach \(\theta_0+(C-C)\). For LoRA, \(\mathcal M_{\le r}\) is closed under negation and \(\mathcal M_{\le r}+\mathcal M_{\le r}=\mathcal M_{\le\min(2r,m,n)}\) by splitting an SVD (C.12), so \(C-C=\mathcal M_{\le\min(2r,m,n)}\), which is why no exact pointing reaches an update of rank above \(2r\) (Thm II.1).

Definition B.15 (strong monoidal functors; \((\mathbb N,+)\) and the permutation groupoid). A strong monoidal functor \(F:(\mathcal C,\otimes,I)\to(\mathcal D,\otimes,J)\) is a functor with isomorphisms \(\phi_{A,B}:FA\otimes FB\to F(A\otimes B)\), natural in \(A\) and \(B\), and \(\phi_0:J\to FI\), compatible with associators and unitors. It is symmetric if also \(F(\sigma_{A,B})\circ\phi_{A,B}=\phi_{B,A}\circ\sigma_{FA,FB}\). The discrete monoidal category \((\mathbb N,+)\) has the natural numbers as objects, only identity morphisms, \(r\otimes s=r+s\) and identity symmetry. The permutation groupoid \(\mathbb P\) has the same objects, the symmetric group \(S_r\) as the automorphisms of \(r\) and no other morphisms, with \(\otimes\) given by \(+\) and block sums of permutations, and the block transposition in \(S_{r+s}\) as symmetry.

Concatenation (B.14) makes \(r\mapsto\mathrm{LoRA}_r\) strong monoidal from \((\mathbb N,+)\) into unpointed methods. It is not symmetric there, because \(\phi_{s,r}\circ\sigma\) lists the blocks in the other order. On \(\mathbb P\) the block transposition acts on \(\mathrm{LoRA}_{r+s}\) by permuting inner channels, \((B,A)\mapsto(BP^{-1},PA)\), and the symmetry square commutes. Pointed objects behave differently. If \(F(r)\) and \(F(2r)\) are standard \(\mathrm T_A\) pointings (\(B_0=0\) and \(A_0\) of full row rank, C.19), which needs \(2r\lt\min(m,n)\), then \(F(r)\oplus F(r)\not\cong F(2r)\) as pointed objects, since \(\dim S_1\) is \(mr\) against \(2mr\) (Obs I.7).

Used in: Obs I.7, The referee record. Reading: Mac Lane (1998), Ch. XI.

Definition B.16 (bicategories and the Para construction). A bicategory has objects, 1-cells between objects and 2-cells between parallel 1-cells. 2-cells compose vertically and horizontally, and composition of 1-cells is associative and unital up to coherent invertible 2-cells. For a symmetric monoidal category \(\mathcal C\), here always cartesian, the bicategory \(\mathbf{Para}(\mathcal C)\) has the objects of \(\mathcal C\). A 1-cell \(X\to Y\) is a pair \((P,\,f:P\times X\to Y)\), and \((P',g):Y\to Z\) composes with it to \((P'\times P,\ (p',p,x)\mapsto g(p',f(p,x)))\). A 2-cell \((P,f)\Rightarrow(Q,g)\) is a reparametrisation \(r:Q\to P\) with \(g=f\circ(r\times\mathrm{id}_X)\). A 2-functor (pseudofunctor) between bicategories sends objects, 1-cells and 2-cells to objects, 1-cells and 2-cells, and preserves both compositions up to coherent invertible 2-cells.

A linear layer is the 1-cell \(\big(\mathbb R^{m\times n},\,(W,x)\mapsto Wx\big):\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m\), and stacking a second layer \(\mathbb R^m\to\mathbb R^k\) composes 1-cells, with parameter space \(\mathbb R^{k\times m}\times\mathbb R^{m\times n}\). A pretrained model is a pointed 1-cell \(F=(\Theta,\theta_0,f)\) of \(\mathbf{Para}(\mathbf{Diff})\), and its realisation \(\Phi_f:\Theta\to C^\infty(X,Y)\), \(\theta\mapsto f(\theta,-)\), records the function at each weight. LoRA is a 2-cell \(F\Rightarrow(Q,f\circ(\rho\times\mathrm{id}_X))\), the network with \((B,A)\) in its parameter slot, and a simulation is a 2-cell between two such cells (Remark I.3). An adapter extension is a 2-cell into \(F\), and merging it is a lift through \(\Phi_f\) (Def VI.1). Composition is associative only up to \((P''\times P')\times P\cong P''\times(P'\times P)\), hence a bicategory; over a strict monoidal category it is a 2-category.

Used in: Def I.1, What Para adds, Remark I.3, Def VI.1, Backpropagation as a lens, Key references. Reading: Leinster, “Basic bicategories” (1998).

Definition B.17 (lenses, reverse derivatives and \(\mathbf{Para}(\mathsf R)\)). In a cartesian category, a lens \((A,A')\to(B,B')\) is a pair of maps, a forward part \(f:A\to B\) and a backward part \(f^\sharp:A\times B'\to A'\). Lenses compose by \((g,g^\sharp)\circ(f,f^\sharp)=\big(g\circ f,\ (a,c')\mapsto f^\sharp(a,g^\sharp(f(a),c'))\big)\) and form the category \(\mathbf{Lens}(\mathcal C)\). For smooth maps between open subsets of Euclidean spaces, the reverse derivative is \(\mathsf R[f](x,y)=J_f(x)^\top y\). The chain rule \(J_{g\circ f}(x)^\top=J_f(x)^\top J_g(f(x))^\top\) says that \(f\mapsto(f,\mathsf R[f])\) is a functor into \(\mathbf{Lens}\). Applied to parametrised maps it gives the 2-functor \(\mathbf{Para}(\mathsf R):\mathbf{Para}(\mathcal C)\to\mathbf{Para}(\mathbf{Lens}(\mathcal C))\) of Cruttwell et al., which is backpropagation.

LoRA’s forward part is \(\rho(B,A)=W_0+sBA\), and its backward part pulls the weight gradient \(G\) back to \(\mathsf R[\rho]((B,A),G)=(sGA^\top,\,sB^\top G)\). GaLore keeps the forward part \(\mathrm{id}_\Theta\) and uses the compression \(G\mapsto P^\top G\) with anchor \(P\), a lens whose backward part is not the reverse derivative of its forward part (Def IV.1). For linear \(\rho(C)=W_k+\mathcal LC\), \(\mathsf R[\rho](C,G)=\mathcal L^*G\) does not depend on \(C\), which is why the two readings agree at degree one (Prop IV.2).

Used in: Exposé IV, Backpropagation as a lens, Def IV.1, Prop IV.2, Rosetta entry 25, Where the category theory works, Seven borrowed words. Reading: Cruttwell, Gavranović, Ghani, Wilson and Zanasi, “Categorical foundations of gradient-based learning” (arXiv 2021; ESOP 2022).

C. Manifolds, germs and cones

22 entries

Parts A and B used only the arrows of a method and its wiring. Part C turns to its geometry. A PEFT method on this site is a smooth map \(\rho:(Q,q_0)\to(\Theta,\theta_0)\), and its expressivity is read off the set \(\mathrm{Im}\,M=\rho(Q)\) near \(\theta_0\). This part collects the geometry behind that reading, from manifolds, differentials and rank for the map to germs, cones, singular points and semialgebraic dimension for the image, which is often not a manifold.

Manifolds and smooth maps

Definition C.1 (smooth manifolds and smooth maps). A smooth manifold of dimension \(k\) is a space covered by charts, bijections from open pieces of it onto open subsets of \(\mathbb R^k\), whose changes of chart are smooth. Every manifold on this site is an open subset of a vector space or an embedded submanifold of one (C.4), or is diffeomorphic to one, as quotients (D.22) and the Grassmannian (C.15) are. A diffeomorphism is a smooth bijection with smooth inverse, and \(\mathrm{Diff}(Q)\) is the group of diffeomorphisms of \(Q\) under composition. A local diffeomorphism maps an open neighbourhood of each point diffeomorphically onto an open set; by the inverse function theorem, \(\rho\) does this near \(q\) whenever \(d\rho_q\) (C.2) is invertible.

LoRA’s parameter space \(\mathbb R^{m\times r}\times\mathbb R^{r\times n}\) is a vector space, and HRA’s \(\{(u_1,\dots,u_r):u_i\ne0\}\) is open in \((\mathbb R^n)^r\). The rotation group \(SO(n)\) is a compact manifold of dimension \(n(n-1)/2\), and the Cayley map (D.7) is a diffeomorphism from \(\mathfrak{so}(n)\) onto an open dense subset of it. A method is a pointed smooth map, and its gauge group \(\{\varphi\in\mathrm{Diff}(Q):\rho\circ\varphi=\rho\}\) (D.21) contains LoRA’s \((B,A)\mapsto(Bg^{-1},gA)\) for every \(g\in GL_r\).

Used in: Def I.2, Def I.5, Thm II.7, Prop IV.3. Reading: Lee, Introduction to Smooth Manifolds (2nd ed., 2013), Ch. 1 and 2.

Definition C.2 (tangent spaces, differentials and covectors). For a manifold \(Q\subseteq\mathbb R^N\), the tangent space \(T_qQ\) is the set of velocities \(c'(0)\) of smooth curves \(c\) in \(Q\) with \(c(0)=q\). It is a linear space of dimension \(\dim Q\), all of \(\mathbb R^N\) when \(Q\) is open. The differential of a smooth \(\rho\) at \(q\) is the linear map \(d\rho_q:T_qQ\to T_{\rho(q)}\Theta\), \(c'(0)\mapsto(\rho\circ c)'(0)\). In coordinates it is the Jacobian, and the chain rule reads \(d(\rho\circ h)_q=d\rho_{h(q)}\circ dh_q\). The cotangent space \(T^*_\theta\Theta\) is the dual of \(T_\theta\Theta\). The differential \(dL_\theta\) of a loss is a covector, and it becomes a gradient vector \(\nabla L(\theta)\) only once an inner product is chosen.

For LoRA, \(d\rho_{(B,A)}(\dot B,\dot A)=s(\dot BA+B\dot A)\). For a rotation orbit, \(T_I\,SO(n)=\mathfrak{so}(n)\) and \(T_{W_0R}\big(W_0\cdot SO(n)\big)=W_0R\,\mathfrak{so}(n)\). Obs I.6 is the chain rule at \(q_0\). Thm III.5 drives the parameters by any cotangent signal \(\gamma(t)\in T^*_{\rho(q)}\Theta\), pulled back to \(Q\) as \(\gamma\circ d\rho_q\), which is \(D\rho^\top\gamma\) in coordinates.

Used in: Notation, Def I.5, Obs I.6, Thm II.2, Thm II.7, Thm III.5, Def IV.1. Reading: Lee (2013), Ch. 3 and 11.

Definition C.3 (rank, expressive dimension and first-order image). The rank of \(\rho\) at \(q\) is \(\operatorname{rank}d\rho_q\). It is lower semicontinuous, since a nonzero \(k\times k\) minor of the Jacobian stays nonzero nearby, so \(\{q:\operatorname{rank}d\rho_q\ge k\}\) is open. The generic rank is \(\max_q\operatorname{rank}d\rho_q\). If \(\rho\) is real-analytic and \(Q\) is connected, the generic rank is attained on an open dense set whose complement has measure zero (C.22). The site writes \(\lvert M\rvert=\dim Q\) for the parameter budget, \(d(M)\) for the generic rank, \(S_1=d\rho_{q_0}(T_{q_0}Q)\) for the first-order image and \(d(M)-\dim S_1\) for the first-order deficit. For definable \(\rho\) on an open definable \(Q\), as for every method on the site, C.18(d) shows that \(d(M)=\dim\mathrm{Im}\,M\), the expressive dimension. The deficit vanishes exactly when \(q_0\) is a point of maximal rank; the base point \(\theta_0\) is then called regular for \(M\), and an apex when the deficit is positive (Def I.5).

Prop II.3 gives LoRA’s rank at every point, \(m\operatorname{rk}A+n\operatorname{rk}B-\operatorname{rk}A\operatorname{rk}B\). At a random pair it is \(r(m+n-r)=d\); at the zero-init point \((0,A_0)\) it is \(mr\), a deficit of \(r(n-r)\). Since the points of lower rank have measure zero, a float64 Jacobian rank at one random point computes \(d(M)\) with probability one, up to the tolerance that decides numerical rank. The plug workbench and the check script do exactly this.

Used in: Notation, Def I.5, Prop II.3, Prop II.12, Figure 6, reproducing the numbers.

Definition C.4 (immersions, submersions and embedded submanifolds). A smooth \(\rho:Q\to\Theta\) is an immersion if every \(d\rho_q\) is injective (rank \(\dim Q\)), and a submersion if every \(d\rho_q\) is surjective (rank \(\dim\Theta\)). A subset \(X\subseteq\mathbb R^N\) is an embedded submanifold of dimension \(k\) if near each of its points it is the zero set of \(N-k\) smooth functions with linearly independent gradients. A submersion is an open map, so its image contains an open set. An embedding, an injective immersion that is a homeomorphism onto its image, has an embedded submanifold as image; an injective immersion such as the figure eight need not.

The one-sided method \(\mathrm{FA}(A_0):B\mapsto W_0+BA_0\) is an injective immersion with affine image \(W_0+\{BA_0\}\) when \(A_0\) has full row rank. On pairs of full rank, \((B,A)\mapsto BA\) is a submersion onto \(\mathcal M_r\) (C.12). Obs I.4(b) uses openness, in the argument recalled in A.13. Prop IV.3 asks for injective immersions \((\mathbb R^k,0)\to(U,\theta)\) tangent to a distribution.

Used in: Obs I.4, Prop IV.3, integrability. Reading: Lee (2013), Ch. 4 and 5.

Fact C.5 (constant-rank theorem). Let \(\rho:Q\to\Theta\) be smooth with \(\operatorname{rank}d\rho_q=k\) for all \(q\) near \(p\). Then there are charts centred at \(p\) and at \(\rho(p)\) in which \(\rho\) reads \((x_1,\dots,x_{\dim Q})\mapsto(x_1,\dots,x_k,0,\dots,0)\). Hence some neighbourhood \(V\) of \(p\) has \(\rho(V)\) an embedded \(k\)-dimensional submanifold, and \(\rho^{-1}(\rho(p))\cap V\) an embedded submanifold of dimension \(\dim Q-k\). By C.3 the hypothesis holds wherever the rank is maximal.
More: the idea of the proof

Choose coordinates in which the top-left \(k\times k\) block of the Jacobian is invertible. The inverse function theorem straightens the first \(k\) output coordinates. Constant rank then forces the other outputs to depend only on those \(k\), and a second change of coordinates in the target removes them.

The site uses this theorem without naming it. Near a point of maximal rank, \(\rho\) is locally a projection onto a sheet of dimension \(d(M)\), with fibres of dimension \(\lvert M\rvert-d(M)\), the gauge waste. For LoRA at a full-rank pair the sheet is a piece of \(W_0+\mathcal M_r\) and the fibre a piece of a \(GL_r\)-orbit. Prop IV.3 makes the analogous constant-rank assumption for distributions, where Frobenius’ theorem (D.25) takes the place of this one.

Used in: Def I.5, Prop II.12, C.18. Reading: Lee (2013), Ch. 4 (the rank theorem).

Definition C.6 (fibres and fibre dimension). The fibre of \(\rho\) over \(\theta\) is \(\rho^{-1}(\theta)\), the set of parameters that give the weight \(\theta\). If \(\operatorname{rank}d\rho_q=d(M)\), C.5 makes the fibre through \(q\) a submanifold of dimension \(\lvert M\rvert-d(M)\) near \(q\). For real-analytic \(\rho\) on connected \(Q\) this holds for all \(q\) outside a closed set of measure zero, and these are the generic fibres. Fibres through points of lower rank can have a different dimension. The fibres of a fibre bundle all look alike locally, so a map whose fibre dimension jumps is no fibre bundle.

For LoRA on the full-rank stratum, the fibre through \((B,A)\) is the orbit \(\{(Bg^{-1},gA):g\in GL_r\}\), of dimension \(r^2=r(m+n)-r(m+n-r)\) (D.21). For zero-init LoRA the fibre over \(\theta_0\) is \(\{(B,A):BA=0\}\). It contains \(\{B=0\}\), of dimension \(rn\gt r^2\), and is itself a union of strata, such as \(\{(0,A):\operatorname{rank}A=r\}\) (Cor III.13).

Used in: Exposé III, Def I.5, Prop II.12, Cor III.13, Rosetta entries 6 and 11, Seven borrowed words.

Definition C.7 (fibre products, transversality and clean intersection). For \(\rho_M:Q_M\to\Theta\) and \(\rho_N:Q_N\to\Theta\), the set-level fibre product is \(P=\{(q,q'):\rho_M(q)=\rho_N(q')\}\subseteq Q_M\times Q_N\). The maps are transverse, \(\rho_M\pitchfork\rho_N\), if \(d\rho_M(T_qQ_M)+d\rho_N(T_{q'}Q_N)=T_\theta\Theta\) at every \((q,q')\in P\); then \(P\) is an embedded submanifold of dimension \(\lvert M\rvert+\lvert N\rvert-\dim\Theta\), and it is the pullback of \(\rho_M\) and \(\rho_N\) in \(\mathbf{Diff}\) (A.11). The intersection is clean if \(P\) is an embedded submanifold with \(T_{(q,q')}P=\{(v,w):d\rho_Mv=d\rho_Nw\}\) at every point. Transverse maps meet cleanly, and so do affine maps.

Transversality at \((q_0,q_0')\) needs \(\lvert M\rvert+\lvert N\rvert\ge\dim\Theta\), which fails for any two small methods, so the products the site builds come from clean cases. The affine methods \(\mathrm{FA}(A_0)\) and \(\mathrm{FB}(B_0)\), with \(B_0\) of full column rank and \(A_0\) of full row rank, have the linear fibre product \(\{(B_0R,RA_0)\}\) of dimension \(r^2\), which is LoRA-XS (Prop I.8). On \(\Theta=\mathbb R\), \(\mathrm{LoRA}_1\) and the frozen model have the cross \(\{BA=0\}\) as fibre product. It is no manifold at the origin, and no product exists (A.13).

Germs, cones and singular points

Definition C.8 (germs). Two subsets \(X,Y\subseteq\Theta\) have the same germ at \(\theta_0\) if \(X\cap U=Y\cap U\) for some neighbourhood \(U\) of \(\theta_0\); two maps have the same germ at \(q_0\) if they agree near \(q_0\). The germs \((X,\theta_0)\) and \((Y,0)\) are diffeomorphic if a diffeomorphism \(\varphi\) between neighbourhoods \(U\ni\theta_0\) and \(V\ni0\) has \(\varphi(\theta_0)=0\) and \(\varphi(X\cap U)=Y\cap V\). Local properties, such as the dimension at \(\theta_0\), smoothness at \(\theta_0\) and the tangent cone (C.11), depend only on the germ.

The image germ of a method is the germ of \(\mathrm{Im}\,M=\rho(Q)\) at \(\theta_0\). It uses all of \(Q\), so it can be larger than the image of a small neighbourhood of \(q_0\). For zero-init LoRA, \(sBA\) with \(B\) small and \(A\) arbitrary is a small update with any row space, so the germ is that of the whole cone \(\theta_0+\mathcal M_{\le r}\); parameters near \((0,A_0)\) only give updates with row space inside \(\mathrm{row}\,A\) for some \(A\) near \(A_0\). For injective \(W_0\) and even \(r\) with \(2\le r\le n-2\), HRA’s image germ at \(W_0\) is the image of the germ of \(\Sigma_r\) at \(0\) under the embedding \(\Omega\mapsto W_0\,C(\Omega)\) of \(\mathfrak{so}(n)\) into \(\mathbb R^{m\times n}\) (C.13, D.7).

Definition C.9 (smooth and singular points). A point \(x\) of a set \(X\subseteq\mathbb R^N\) is a smooth point if \(X\cap U\) is a \(C^1\) embedded submanifold for some neighbourhood \(U\) of \(x\), and a singular point otherwise. The singular points form the singular locus \(\mathrm{Sing}\,X\). Algebraic geometry defines \(\mathrm{Sing}\) through the Jacobian of defining polynomials, and for \(\mathcal M_{\le r}\) the two notions agree. Smoothness is a property of a set. The site’s regular base point and apex, defined by a zero or positive first-order deficit (C.3, Def I.5), are properties of a method at \(q_0\).

The cross \(\{BA=0\}\subseteq\mathbb R^2\) is singular at the origin and smooth elsewhere. For \(1\le r\lt\min(m,n)\), \(\mathrm{Sing}\,\mathcal M_{\le r}=\mathcal M_{\le r-1}\). A split initialisation has image \((\theta_0-P)+\mathcal M_{\le r}\) with \(\operatorname{rank}P=r\), so \(\theta_0\) is a smooth point; zero-init LoRA starts at the singular vertex. A figure-eight curve passing through its crossing with nonzero velocity has deficit \(0\) there, though its image is singular, so smooth and regular are different notions.

Definition C.10 (cones and vertices). A set \(X\) in a vector space is a cone with vertex \(v\) if \(v\in X\) and \(v+t(x-v)\in X\) for all \(x\in X\) and \(t\ge0\), and linear if it is an affine subspace. If \(\rho=\theta_0+\delta\) with \(\delta(tu,w)=t\,\delta(u,w)\) for \(t\ge0\) on a block \(u\) of parameters, and \(Q\) is closed under \(u\mapsto tu\), then \(\mathrm{Im}\,M\) is a cone with vertex \(\theta_0\). A cone that is a \(C^1\) embedded submanifold near its vertex \(v\) equals \(v+T_vX\), so the vertex of a non-linear cone is a singular point.
More: why a smooth cone is linear

Each ray from \(v\) lies in \(X\), so \(X\subseteq v+T_vX\). Near \(v\), \(X\) is a submanifold of full dimension in this affine space, so it contains a neighbourhood of \(v\) there, and scaling spreads it over all of \(v+T_vX\).

\(\mathcal M_{\le r}\) is a closed cone with vertex \(0\), and it is not linear for \(1\le r\lt\min(m,n)\), since a rank-\(r\) and a rank-one matrix can sum to rank \(r+1\). Zero-init LoRA has \(\delta=sBA\), linear in \(B\), so its image \(W_0+\mathcal M_{\le r}\) has singular vertex \(W_0\). Its deficit \(r(n-r)\) makes \(\theta_0\) an apex too; the vertex is a property of the set, the apex one of the method. Multihomogeneous updates such as LoHa’s give cones in the same way (D.19).

Fact C.11 (first-order image versus image). The tangent cone of a set \(X\) at \(x\) is the set of limits of \(t_k(x_k-x)\) with \(x_k\in X\), \(x_k\to x\) and \(t_k\gt0\); for a closed cone with vertex \(v\) it is \(X-v\). For a method \(M\): (a) \(S_1\) lies in the tangent cone of \(\mathrm{Im}\,M\) at \(\theta_0\); (b) \(\dim S_1\le d(M)\); (c) if \(\mathrm{Im}\,M\) is a \(C^1\) manifold of dimension \(d(M)\) near \(\theta_0\), then \(S_1\subseteq T_{\theta_0}\mathrm{Im}\,M\), with equality exactly when the deficit is \(0\).

For (a), \(d\rho_{q_0}(\xi)\) is the velocity of the curve \(\rho(q_0+t\xi)\) inside the image; for (c), \(\rho\) maps a neighbourhood of \(q_0\) into the manifold. For \(\mathrm T_A\)-pointed LoRA (\(B_0=0\), C.19) the tangent cone at \(\theta_0\) is \(\mathcal M_{\le r}\) itself, and inside it \(S_1=\{XA_0\}\), the matrices with row space in \(\mathrm{row}\,A_0\), is a linear space of dimension \(mr\lt r(m+n-r)\). Figure 2 draws \(S_1\) as one ruling of the cone. At a split pointing case (c) applies, and \(S_1=T_P\mathcal M_r\).

Matrix varieties

Definition C.12 (the determinantal variety). \(\mathcal M_{\le r}=\{X\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}:\operatorname{rank}X\le r\}\), the determinantal variety, is the zero set of all \((r+1)\times(r+1)\) minors, a closed cone with vertex \(0\). Let \(1\le r\lt\min(m,n)\). The matrices of rank exactly \(r\) form an open dense subset \(\mathcal M_r\) of \(\mathcal M_{\le r}\), an embedded submanifold of dimension \(r(m+n-r)\), and \(\mathrm{Sing}\,\mathcal M_{\le r}=\mathcal M_{\le r-1}\). If \(X\in\mathcal M_r\) has column space \(U\) and row space \(V\), and \(X=BA\) with \(B\in\mathbb R^{m\times r}\) and \(A\in\mathbb R^{r\times n}\), then \[T_X\mathcal M_r=\{\dot BA+B\dot A\}=\{D:P_{U^\perp}DP_{V^\perp}=0\}.\] Also \(\mathcal M_{\le r}-\mathcal M_{\le r}=\mathcal M_{\le\min(2r,m,n)}\).

Zero-init LoRA has image \(W_0+\mathcal M_{\le r}\), of dimension \(130{,}816\) for a \(4096\times4096\) matrix at \(r=16\). On full-rank pairs, \((B,A)\mapsto BA\) is a submersion onto \(\mathcal M_r\) whose fibres are \(GL_r\)-orbits, which identifies \(\mathcal M_r\) with the quotient \((\mathbb R^{m\times r}_*\times\mathbb R^{r\times n}_*)/GL_r\) (D.22). The difference formula is why no pointing of \(\mathrm{LoRA}_r\) reaches an update of rank above \(2r\) (B.14, Thm II.1).

Definition C.13 (the skew-determinantal cone). Every skew-symmetric matrix has even rank. For even \(r\) with \(2\le r\le n-2\), the skew-determinantal cone \(\Sigma_r=\{\Omega\in\mathfrak{so}(n):\operatorname{rank}\Omega\le r\}\) is a closed cone with vertex \(0\) and dimension \(r(n-r)+r(r-1)/2=rn-r(r+1)/2\), counting a range in \(\mathrm{Gr}(r,n)\) and a nondegenerate skew form on it. Its smooth points are the matrices of rank exactly \(r\), and \(\mathrm{Sing}\,\Sigma_r=\Sigma_{r-2}\). It is not linear, since skew matrices of rank \(r\) and \(2\) can sum to rank \(r+2\le n\), so its vertex is singular.

For injective \(W_0\) and even \(r\), HRA’s image near \(W_0\) consists of the \(W_0R\) with \(R\in SO(n)\) near \(I\) and \(\operatorname{rank}(R-I)\le r\). The Cayley map satisfies \(\operatorname{rank}(C(\Omega)-I)=\operatorname{rank}\Omega\) (D.7), so the image germ at \(W_0\) is the image of the germ of \(\Sigma_r\) at \(0\) under the embedding \(\Omega\mapsto W_0\,C(\Omega)\), \(W_0\) is a singular vertex, and \(d(\mathrm{HRA}_r)=\dim\Sigma_r\). Then \(S_1=W_0\cdot\mathcal S\) with \[\mathcal S=\{\Omega\in\mathfrak{so}(n):P_{U^\perp}\Omega P_{U^\perp}=0\}=U\wedge\mathbb R^n\] (D.5), a linear space of skew matrices of rank at most \(2k=r\) and of dimension \(kn-k(k+1)/2\lt d(\mathrm{HRA}_r)\). It lies inside \(\Sigma_r\), so \(S_1\) lies inside the tangent cone \(W_0\cdot\Sigma_r\) (Thm II.7(b)).

Definition C.14 (Segre and toric cones). The Segre cone in \(\mathbb R^p\otimes\mathbb R^q\cong\mathbb R^{p\times q}\) is the set \(\{a\otimes b\}=\mathcal M_{\le1}\) of rank-one tensors, a closed cone of dimension \(p+q-1\) that spans the whole space and is non-linear when \(p,q\ge2\). It is the simplest toric cone, the closure of the image of a torus \((\mathbb R^\times)^k\) under a monomial map, here \((a,b)\mapsto(a_ib_j)_{ij}\). Since rank-one matrices span \(\mathbb R^{p\times q}\), \(k\) generic rank-one matrices span \(\min(k,pq)\) dimensions.

Under the Van Loan–Pitsianis rearrangement (B.11), KronA’s update \(A\otimes B\) becomes \(\mathrm{vec}(A)\,\mathrm{vec}(B)^\top\), so its image is a linear copy of a Segre cone (Prop II.12). VeRA’s update \(\Lambda_bB\Lambda_dA\) has entries \(\sum_k(b_id_k)B_{ik}A_{kj}\), a fixed linear function of the rank-one matrix \(bd^\top\). This function is injective when \(B\) has no zero entry and \(A\) has full row rank, so the image is a toric cone of dimension \(m+r-1\) with vertex \(\theta_0\), as VeRA’s card records. Prop II.8 uses the spanning fact for DoRA, with \(m\) points of the Segre cone in \(\mathbb R^{m-r}\otimes\mathbb R^{n-r}\).

Used in: Prop II.8, Prop II.12, VeRA. Reading: Harris (1992), Lecture 2; Cox, Little and Schenck, Toric Varieties (2011), Ch. 1.

Definition C.15 (the Grassmannian). The Grassmannian \(\mathrm{Gr}(r,n)\) is the set of \(r\)-dimensional linear subspaces of \(\mathbb R^n\), a compact manifold of dimension \(r(n-r)\). One model is the full-row-rank \(r\times n\) matrices up to \(A\mapsto gA\), \(g\in GL_r\), through \(A\mapsto\mathrm{row}\,A\); another is the orthogonal projectors \(P=P^\top=P^2\) with \(\operatorname{tr}P=r\). Near \(V\), the graphs of linear maps \(V\to V^\perp\) give a chart onto \(\mathrm{Hom}(V,V^\perp)\cong\mathbb R^{r(n-r)}\). \(\mathrm{Gr}(r,n)\) carries a unique \(O(n)\)-invariant probability measure, the uniform or Haar measure, and the row space of a Gaussian \(A\) has this law (D.14).

\(\mathrm T_A\)-pointed LoRA is classified up to isomorphism by \(\mathrm{row}\,A_0\in\mathrm{Gr}(r,n)\) (Thm II.2), and its deficit \(r(n-r)\) equals \(\dim\mathrm{Gr}(r,n)\). First steps keep the row space of the update inside \(\mathrm{row}\,A_0\), and the missing directions are those that tilt it. For injective \(W_0\) and even \(r\) with \(2\le r\le n-2\), HRA’s image is the union of the orbits \(W_0\cdot SO(V)\) over \(V\in\mathrm{Gr}(r,n)\), and its dimension \(r(n-r)+r(r-1)/2\) counts a subspace and a rotation inside it. LoRA-FA, which trains \(B\) with \(A_0\) frozen, sees \(A_0\) only through \(V=\mathrm{row}\,A_0\) once its gradient is corrected as in Prop III.4(c).

Used in: Thm II.2, checks, Thm II.7, LOFT, LOFT example, Prop III.4, Cor III.13. Reading: Lee (2013), Ch. 1; Harris (1992), Lecture 6.

Tame sets and genericity

Fact C.16 (images of smooth maps need not be manifolds). Even a polynomial map can have an image that is no manifold. (a) Cusps. \(t\mapsto(t^2,t^3)\) is injective with image \(\{y^2=x^3\}\), which is not \(C^1\) at \(0\). (b) Crossings and cones. The cross \(\{BA=0\}\subseteq\mathbb R^2\) and the cone \(\mathcal M_{\le r}\) are singular at the origin. (c) Missing limits. \((x,y)\mapsto(x,xy)\) has image \(\{u\ne0\}\cup\{(0,0)\}\) in the \((u,v)\)-plane, neither open nor closed. (d) Dense images. The line of irrational slope is an injective immersion into the torus with dense image.

PEFT images show (b) routinely, as in the LoRA cone and HRA’s skew cone, so the site measures them with tools that need no manifold structure, such as germs (C.8), tangent cones (C.11) and the dimension theory of semialgebraic and definable sets (C.17, C.18). Whether LoHa’s Hadamard image is closed, the question raised by (c), is open.

Used in: Obs I.4, Thm II.2, Thm II.7, open problems.

Definition C.17 (semialgebraic and definable sets). A semialgebraic set is a finite union of sets \(\{x\in\mathbb R^N:p(x)=0,\ q_1(x)\gt0,\dots,q_k(x)\gt0\}\) with polynomials \(p,q_i\), and a map is semialgebraic if its graph is. A set is definable in \(\mathbb R_{\exp}\) if a first-order formula defines it from polynomials with real coefficients, \(\exp\), equality, order, Boolean operations and quantifiers over \(\mathbb R\). Wilkie proved that \(\mathbb R_{\exp}\) is o-minimal, meaning every definable subset of \(\mathbb R\) is a finite union of points and intervals. Semialgebraic sets are the definable sets that need no \(\exp\).

LoRA, DoRA (whose row norms involve square roots), HRA and exact Cayley OFT are built from polynomials, quotients and square roots, so their images are semialgebraic by C.18(a). A softmax brings in \(\exp\), and maps that contain one are definable in \(\mathbb R_{\exp}\).

Used in: where the category theory works, arrow rules. Reading: Bochnak, Coste and Roy, Real Algebraic Geometry (1998), Ch. 2; Wilkie, J. Amer. Math. Soc. 9 (1996).

Fact C.18 (Tarski–Seidenberg and definable dimension). (a) The image of a semialgebraic set under a semialgebraic map is semialgebraic (Tarski–Seidenberg); images of definable sets under definable maps are definable. (b) Every definable set \(X\) is a finite disjoint union of \(C^1\) embedded submanifolds, and \(\dim X\), the largest of their dimensions, does not depend on the choice. (c) \(X\subseteq Y\) implies \(\dim X\le\dim Y\), \(\dim f(X)\le\dim X\) for definable \(f\), and \(\dim(\overline X\setminus X)\lt\dim X\) for nonempty \(X\). (d) For a definable \(C^1\) map \(\rho\) on an open definable \(Q\subseteq\mathbb R^k\), \(\dim\rho(Q)=\max_q\operatorname{rank}d\rho_q\).
More: the idea of (d)

Near a point of maximal rank, C.5 gives a sheet of that dimension inside \(\rho(Q)\). Conversely, a definable section of \(\rho\), \(C^1\) on a top-dimensional piece of \(\rho(Q)\), composes with \(\rho\) to the identity there, so the rank is at least that dimension.

Part (d) is the identity \(d(M)=\dim\mathrm{Im}\,M\) of Def I.5. Part (c) is the monotonicity behind test O2, since an arrow \(M\to N\) gives \(\mathrm{Im}\,M\subseteq\mathrm{Im}\,N\) and hence \(d(M)\le d(N)\); for instance \(\mathrm{LoRA}_r\not\to\) LoRA-FA because \(r(m+n-r)\gt mr\).

Used in: Def I.5, arrow rules, where the category theory works. Reading: van den Dries, Tame Topology and O-minimal Structures (1998), Ch. 3 and 4; Bochnak, Coste and Roy (1998), Ch. 2.

Definition C.19 (stratification and local dimension). A stratification of a set \(X\) is a partition into finitely many embedded submanifolds, the strata, such that the closure of each stratum is a union of strata. The local dimension \(\dim_xX\) is the dimension of the germ of \(X\) at \(x\), the largest dimension of a stratum whose closure contains \(x\). \(X\) is pure-dimensional if \(\dim_xX\) is the same at every point. Definable sets admit stratifications, so these notions make sense for every image on the site.

The cross \(\{ba=0\}\subseteq\mathbb R^2\) is stratified as the origin and the four open half-axes, and it is pure of dimension \(1\). Likewise \(\mathcal M_{\le r}=\mathcal M_0\sqcup\mathcal M_1\sqcup\dots\sqcup\mathcal M_r\) with \(\overline{\mathcal M_k}=\mathcal M_{\le k}\), and it is pure of dimension \(r(m+n-r)\). For \(1\le r\lt\min(m,n)\), Thm II.2 singles out three strata of full-rank LoRA pointings, \(\mathrm T_A\) (\(B_0=0\), \(\operatorname{rank}A_0=r\)), \(\mathrm T_B\) (\(A_0=0\), \(\operatorname{rank}B_0=r\)) and the open dense \(\mathrm S\) (\(\operatorname{rank}B_0A_0=r\)), and the fibre \(\{BA=0\}\) over \(\theta_0\) is itself stratified (Cor III.13). The HRA fibre product of Thm II.7(d) has local dimension \(d(\mathrm{HRA}_r)+r^2=rn+r(r-1)/2\) over generic points of the meet and contains \(\{(B,0,I)\}\), of dimension \(mr\), over \(W_0\), so it is not pure-dimensional when \(mr\gt rn+r(r-1)/2\).

Used in: Thm II.2, three strata, Thm II.7, Cor III.13, open problems, Seven borrowed words. Reading: van den Dries (1998), Ch. 4; Bochnak, Coste and Roy (1998), Ch. 9.

Definition C.20 (moduli sets and moduli spaces). Classifying objects up to isomorphism gives a set of classes. A moduli space also carries a topology or geometry for which continuous or smooth families of objects correspond to continuous or smooth maps into it, ideally with a universal family over it from which every family is pulled back. A disjoint union of spaces \(X\sqcup Y\) has the topology in which \(X\) and \(Y\) are both open and closed.

Thm II.2 classifies the three strata of LoRA pointings, as a set, by \(\mathrm{Gr}(r,n)\sqcup\mathrm{Gr}(r,m)\sqcup\mathcal M_r\). The quotient topology does not split this way, since \((\varepsilon B',A_0)\in\mathrm S\) tends to \((0,A_0)\in\mathrm T_A\) as \(\varepsilon\to0\), so \(\mathrm T_A\) lies in the closure of \(\mathrm S\). No universal family is built, and the site uses “moduli” as an analogy.

Used in: Thm II.2, Rosetta, Seven borrowed words. Reading: Harris (1992), Lecture 4.

Definition C.21 (generic properties). A property of points of \(\mathbb R^N\) holds generically if the set where it fails lies in \(\{f=0\}\) for some nonzero polynomial \(f\). The set \(\{f=0\}\) is closed, nowhere dense and of Lebesgue measure zero, so a generic property holds with probability one at a random point whose law has a density, such as a Gaussian or uniform initialisation. For points of a parametrised set, such as a Segre cone, “generic” refers to generic parameters. To prove genericity it suffices to write failure as the vanishing of polynomials and exhibit one point where one of them is nonzero. A weaker notion, almost every point, asks only that the failure set have Lebesgue measure zero, as a countable union of proper algebraic subsets does.

Thm II.2(d) uses a generic rank-one update, Prop II.8 gives DoRA’s dimension \(\min\big(mn,\,r(m+n-r)+m\big)\) for generic \(W_0\), and Prop V.2 notes that QLoRA’s defect \(e=\kappa W-W\) has full rank for almost every \(W\), in the weaker sense. The quantiser \(\kappa\) is defined cell by cell rather than by one polynomial, so the argument runs cell by cell, and the failure set is a countable union of proper algebraic subsets of quantisation cells, which has measure zero. A pretrained \(W_0\) is not random, and structured weights can lie in the exceptional set. For a rank-one \(W_0=ac^\top\) with all \(a_i\ne0\) and \(r\lt n\), Prop II.8 finds DoRA’s dimension \(r(m+n-r)+m-r\), strictly below the generic value exactly when \(r\lt m\) and \(r\le n-2\).

Used in: Thm II.2, Prop II.8, Thm II.11, Prop II.12, Prop V.2.

Definition C.22 (real-analytic functions and their zero sets). A function is real-analytic on an open set if near every point it equals a convergent power series. Polynomials, \(\exp\), softmax, \(\tanh\), GELU and SiLU are real-analytic, and so are sums, products, compositions and quotients with nonvanishing denominator. On a connected open set, a real-analytic function that vanishes on a nonempty open subset vanishes identically, and the zero set of one that is not identically zero is closed, nowhere dense and of measure zero.

Lemma VI.2 compares Taylor coefficients at \(0\). A nonzero scale \(c\) with \(\sigma(ct)=c\,\sigma(t)\) must satisfy \(ca_k=c^ka_k\) for every coefficient \(a_k\) of \(\sigma\), so \(c=1\) for GELU and SiLU, whose \(a_2\ne0\). Thm VI.5(f) uses the zero-set fact. A prefixed head is analytic in the prefix, and one prefix with a nonzero second derivative makes the prefixes with affine output a nowhere-dense set. The same fact makes the maximal-rank set of an analytic \(\rho\) open and dense (C.3).

Part D turns to symmetry. Most PEFT parametrisations have symmetries, since different trainable parameters can give the same weights and some transformations of weight space carry a method’s image to itself. Definition D.2 (tori, diagonal monoids and saturation). The diagonal torus \(T_m=\{\mathrm{diag}(d):d_i\ne0\}\cong(\mathbb R^\times)^m\) is a commutative Lie group with \(2^m\) connected components, and \(T_m^+=\{\mathrm{diag}(d):d_i\gt0\}\cong\mathbb R_{\gt0}^m\) is the component of the identity. \(\mathrm{Diag}_m\), the set of all diagonal matrices including singular ones, is a monoid under multiplication (D.10) whose invertible elements form \(T_m\). Identifying \(\mathbb R^2\) with \(\mathbb C\), the scaled rotations \(a\,\mathrm{Rot}(\theta)\) with \(a\gt0\) form \(CO^+(2)\cong\mathbb C^\times\), through \(a\,\mathrm{Rot}(\theta)\mapsto ae^{i\theta}\). If a group or monoid \(G\) acts on a space containing a set \(X\), the saturation \(G\cdot X=\{g\cdot x:g\in G,\ x\in X\}\) is the smallest \(G\)-stable set containing \(X\).

(IA)\(^3\) on keys and values maps \(\ell\mapsto\mathrm{diag}(\ell)W_0\), so its image \(\mathrm{Diag}_m\cdot W_0\) is the closure of the torus orbit \(T_m\cdot W_0\). DoRA’s image is the saturation \(\mathrm{Diag}_m\cdot(W_0+\mathcal M_{\le r})\) of LoRA’s cone. RoAd\(_1\) is the left action of \(CO^+(2)^{m/2}\cong(\mathbb C^\times)^{m/2}\) on pairs of output rows, which makes it (IA)\(^3\) over \(\mathbb C\). LoHa’s gauge contains a two-sided diagonal torus.

Definition D.3 (permutations, monomial matrices, semidirect products, normalisers). \(S_d\) is the group of permutations of \(d\) objects, realised by permutation matrices. A matrix is monomial if each row and each column has exactly one nonzero entry. Every monomial matrix factors uniquely as \(DP\) with \(D\in T_m\) and \(P\) a permutation matrix, and \(T_m\) is normal in the group \(\mathrm{Mon}_m\) of monomial matrices, so \(\mathrm{Mon}_m=T_m\rtimes S_m\). Here a semidirect product \(N\rtimes H\) is a group with a normal subgroup \(N\) and a subgroup \(H\) such that \(N\cap H=\{e\}\) and \(NH\) is the whole group. The signed permutations \(B_r=\{\pm1\}^r\rtimes S_r=\mathrm{Mon}_r\cap O(r)\) are the monomial matrices with entries \(\pm1\), and \(\lvert B_r\rvert=2^rr!\). The normaliser of a subset \(H\subseteq G\) is \(N_G(H)=\{g\in G:gHg^{-1}=H\}\).

A monomial \(g\) satisfies \(g\,\mathrm{Diag}_m\,g^{-1}=\mathrm{Diag}_m\), and in \(GL_m\) the normaliser of \(T_m\) is exactly \(\mathrm{Mon}_m\). This puts \(\mathrm{Mon}_m\times GL_n\) inside the covariance group (D.17) of output scalings such as (IA)\(^3\) and DoRA’s magnitude. Definition D.4 (connectedness and the identity component). The identity component \(G^\circ\) of a Lie group \(G\) is the connected component containing \(e\). It is an open normal subgroup. \(GL_n\) has two components, told apart by the sign of the determinant, and \(GL^+(n)=\{\det\gt0\}\) is the identity component; likewise \(SO(n)\subset O(n)\) and \(T_m^+\subset T_m\). A subset \(U\) is symmetric if \(U^{-1}=U\). Every neighbourhood of \(e\) contains a symmetric one, and in a connected group a symmetric neighbourhood \(U\) of \(e\) generates the group as a monoid, so every element is a finite product \(u_1\cdots u_k\) with \(u_i\in U\).

In merge-and-restart of a method of action type (D.12), each cycle applies one element of a set \(S\). If \(S\) contains a neighbourhood of the identity in a connected group \(H\), repeated merging reaches all of \(H\) (Thm V.3(b)). HF’s Cayley–Neumann OFT factors have \(\det R\ge0\), so the invertible ones lie in \(GL^+(b)\); they generate \(\prod GL^+(b)\) as a group, or \(\prod CO^+(2)\) when \(b=2\).

Used in: Complete invariants, Thm V.3, Corollaries of Thm V.3.

Definition D.5 (Lie algebras; the commutator; \(\mathfrak{gl}\), \(\mathfrak{so}\), \(\mathrm{Sym}\), \(\wedge\)). The Lie algebra of a matrix group \(G\subseteq GL_n\) is \(\mathfrak g=\{X:e^{tX}\in G\text{ for all }t\in\mathbb R\}\), the tangent space of \(G\) at \(I\). It is a vector space closed under the commutator \([X,Y]=XY-YX\). The site uses \(\mathfrak{gl}_r\) (all \(r\times r\) matrices, for \(GL_r\)), \(\mathfrak{so}(n)\) (skew matrices \(\Omega^\top=-\Omega\), for \(O(n)\) and \(SO(n)\)), the diagonal matrices (for \(T_m\)), \(\mathbb RI\oplus\mathfrak{so}(n)\) (for \(CO(n)\)) and \(\mathfrak{sp}(2r)=\{X:X^\top J+JX=0\}\). For a subspace \(V\subseteq\mathbb R^n\), \(\mathfrak{so}(V)\) consists of the skew maps of \(V\) extended by \(0\) on \(V^\perp\). The symmetric matrices \(\mathrm{Sym}_r\) do not form a Lie algebra, since \([S,S']\) is skew. Write \(v\wedge w=vw^\top-wv^\top\in\mathfrak{so}(n)\), \(U\wedge V\) for the span of the \(u\wedge v\) with \(u\in U\), \(v\in V\), and \(L_{ab}=e_a\wedge e_b=E_{ab}-E_{ba}\). Then \([L_{ac},L_{cb}]=L_{ab}\) for distinct \(a,b,c\).

For injective \(W_0\), at HRA’s paired initialisation \(u_{2i-1}=u_{2i}=w_i\) (\(i\le k=r/2\), with independent \(w_i\) and \(U=\mathrm{span}\{w_i\}\) of dimension \(k\)), the first-order image is \(S_1=W_0\cdot\{\Omega\in\mathfrak{so}(n):P_{U^\perp}\Omega P_{U^\perp}=0\}=W_0\cdot(U\wedge\mathbb R^n)\), a subspace of \(W_0\cdot\mathfrak{so}(n)\) of dimension \(kn-k(k+1)/2\) (C.13). LOFT trains a skew \(E\in\mathfrak{so}(r)\) inside a fixed \(r\)-dimensional input subspace. The bracket identity lets rotations of overlapping coordinate blocks generate rotations of their union (D.8).

Definition D.6 (the exponential map; one-parameter subgroups). For \(X\in\mathfrak g\), the curve \(t\mapsto e^{tX}\) of matrix exponentials is a one-parameter subgroup, a smooth homomorphism \((\mathbb R,+)\to G\), and every continuous one-parameter subgroup has this form for a unique \(X\), its generator. The exponential map \(\mathfrak g\to G\) has derivative \(\mathrm{id}\) at \(0\), so it maps a neighbourhood of \(0\) diffeomorphically onto a neighbourhood of \(I\). It is onto for \(SO(n)\) and not onto for \(GL^+(n)\), since \(\mathrm{diag}(-1,-2)\) is \(e^X\) for no real \(X\).

The one-parameter subgroup \(t\mapsto(Be^{-tX},e^{tX}A)\) of LoRA’s gauge moves along a fibre without changing \(BA\). Thm III.5 builds its isometries as \(k=e^{\tau\xi}\) from generators \(\xi\) with \(\Lambda^{-1}\xi\) antisymmetric. LOFT may use the exponential as its map \(\mathfrak{so}(r)\to SO(r)\), and the Kempf–Ness argument (D.30) differentiates the norm along \(e^{t\xi}\).

Definition D.7 (the Cayley map and its Neumann truncation). For skew \(\Omega\) the eigenvalues are imaginary, so \(I-\Omega\) is invertible, and the Cayley map is \[C(\Omega)=(I+\Omega)(I-\Omega)^{-1}.\] It lands in \(SO(n)\), and \(C(\Omega)-I=2\Omega(I-\Omega)^{-1}\), so \(\operatorname{rank}(C(\Omega)-I)=\operatorname{rank}\Omega\). Its expansion \(C(\Omega)=I+2\Omega+2\Omega^2+\cdots\) shows that its derivative at \(0\) is \(2\,\mathrm{id}\). It is a diffeomorphism from \(\mathfrak{so}(n)\) onto the open dense set of rotations with no eigenvalue \(-1\), with inverse \(R\mapsto(R-I)(R+I)^{-1}\), so it misses, for instance, \(\mathrm{diag}(-1,-1,1,\dots,1)\).

Exact-Cayley OFT and block OFT are orthogonal, so they preserve \(K_{\rm out}=WW^\top\) (D.15) and the spectrum. HF PEFT’s default (since version 0.18) truncates \((I-Q)^{-1}\) to \(I+Q+Q^2+Q^3\), which gives \(R=C(Q)(I-Q^4)\). As \(Q^4\) is symmetric and commutes with \(C(Q)\), \(R^\top R=(I-Q^4)^2\ne I\), and for small \(Q\) each merge shrinks \(K_{\rm out}\). The rank identity is how Thm II.7(c) identifies HRA’s image germ with the cone of skew matrices of rank at most \(r\).

Definition D.8 (Lie subgroups and subalgebras; the generated Lie algebra). A Lie subgroup of \(G\) is a subgroup with a Lie group structure for which the inclusion is an injective immersion; it need not be closed. Connected Lie subgroups \(H\subseteq G\) correspond one to one to Lie subalgebras \(\mathfrak h\subseteq\mathfrak g\), with \(H\) the subgroup generated by \(e^{\mathfrak h}\). The Lie algebra generated by subalgebras \(\mathfrak h_1,\dots,\mathfrak h_p\), written \(\mathrm{Lie}\langle\mathfrak h_1,\dots,\mathfrak h_p\rangle\), is the smallest subalgebra containing them, the span of their elements and all iterated brackets of them.

Block OFT, BOFT factors and Givens rotations use \(\mathfrak{so}(I_j)\) for coordinate blocks \(I_j\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}\). Since \([L_{ac},L_{cb}]=L_{ab}\), two overlapping blocks generate \(\mathfrak{so}\) of their union, and \(\mathrm{Lie}\langle\mathfrak{so}(I_j)\rangle_j=\bigoplus_C\mathfrak{so}(C)\), summed over the connected components \(C\) of the hypergraph with vertices \(\{1,\dots,n\}\) and hyperedges \(I_j\). Two vertices share a component when a chain of hyperedges, each meeting the next, joins them. Butterfly factors connect this hypergraph only at full depth.

Used in: Thm V.3, Figure 11, Corollaries of Thm V.3, LOFT fixes one leaf. Reading: Lee (2013), Ch. 19; Hall (2015), Ch. 5.

Fact D.9 (Yamabe’s theorem). Every path-connected subgroup of a Lie group is a Lie subgroup. Consequently, if \(H_1,\dots,H_p\) are connected Lie subgroups of \(G\), the subgroup they generate is the connected Lie subgroup \(H\) with \(\mathrm{Lie}(H)=\mathrm{Lie}\langle\mathfrak h_1,\dots,\mathfrak h_p\rangle\). (H. Yamabe, Osaka Math. J. 2, 1950; a short proof is in M. Goto, Proc. AMS 20, 1969.)

The subgroup generated by the \(H_i\) is path-connected, because products of paths are paths, so the theorem makes it a Lie subgroup and the correspondence of D.8 names it. With D.4, this is why a re-merged action method whose cycles contain a neighbourhood of the identity in each \(H_i\) reaches exactly the connected subgroup with Lie algebra \(\mathrm{Lie}\langle\mathfrak h_1,\dots,\mathfrak h_p\rangle\), and why exact-Cayley block-orthogonal factors reach \(\prod_CSO(C)\).

Used in: Thm V.3, Corollaries of Thm V.3.

Definition D.10 (monoids, units, monoid orbits, homomorphisms). A monoid is a set with an associative product and an identity. Its invertible elements form its group of units. The submonoid generated by a subset \(S\) consists of all finite products \(s_1\cdots s_k\) of elements of \(S\), the empty product being the identity. A monoid acts on a set when \(1\cdot x=x\) and \(s\cdot(t\cdot x)=(st)\cdot x\), and the monoid orbit of \(x\) is \(\{s\cdot x\}\). A homomorphism of monoids preserves products and the identity. Examples are \(\mathrm{Diag}_m\), \(M_2(\mathbb R)\) and \(\mathrm{End}(\Theta)\) under multiplication, with units \(T_m\), \(GL_2(\mathbb R)\) and \(GL(\Theta)\), and finite multisets under disjoint union \(\uplus\). Additive monoids \(\mathrm{Mon}(C)\) are in B.14.

Merge-and-restart reaches the orbit of the monoid that one cycle generates, which is \(\theta_0+\mathrm{Mon}(C)\) for an additive method \(\rho=\theta_0+\delta\) with shape \(C=\mathrm{Im}\,\delta\) (B.14) and \(\langle S\rangle\cdot\theta_0\) for a method of action type (D.12) whose cycle applies elements of \(S\). RoAd\(_2\)’s blocks range over \(M_2(\mathbb R)\). For a fixed query, attention’s pair (total weight, weighted sum of values) is a homomorphism from multisets of key–value pairs to \((\mathbb R_{\ge0}\times\mathbb R^{d_v},+)\) (D.34).

Fact D.11 (Cartan–Dieudonné theorem; Scherk’s sharp form). For nonzero \(u\in\mathbb R^n\), the reflection \(H_u=I-2uu^\top/\|u\|^2\) fixes the hyperplane \(u^\perp\) and has \(\det H_u=-1\). Every \(R\in O(n)\) is a product of at most \(n\) reflections (Cartan–Dieudonné). In Scherk’s sharp form, if \(k=\operatorname{rank}(R-I)\), then \(R\) is a product of exactly \(k\) reflections and of no fewer. Hence \(\det R=(-1)^k\), and every \(R\in SO(n)\) is a product of \(\operatorname{rank}(R-I)\) reflections, an even number. (P. Scherk, Proc. AMS 1, 1950; E. Artin, Geometric Algebra, 1957, Ch. III.)
More: idea of the proof

A product of \(j\) reflections fixes an intersection of \(j\) hyperplanes, so \(\operatorname{rank}(R-I)\le j\). Conversely, pick \(x\) with \(Rx\ne x\) and put \(u=Rx-x\). Then \(H_u\) swaps \(Rx\) and \(x\) and fixes every fixed vector of \(R\), so \(H_uR\) fixes one more dimension, and induction on \(k\) finishes.

HRA multiplies \(r\) reflections, so \(\operatorname{rank}(R-I)\le r\), and \(\det R=1\) for even \(r\). Scherk’s form gives the converse. For injective \(W_0\) and even \(r\), HRA reaches all of \((W_0+\mathcal M_{\le r})\cap W_0\cdot SO(n)\), padding with pairs \(H_uH_u=I\), and re-merged HRA reaches \(W_0\cdot SO(n)\) within \(\lceil n/r\rceil\) cycles.

Actions, orbits and invariants

Definition D.12 (group actions). A (left) action of a group \(G\) on a set \(X\) is a map \((g,x)\mapsto g\cdot x\) with \(e\cdot x=x\) and \(g\cdot(h\cdot x)=(gh)\cdot x\). A right action \(x\cdot g\) satisfies \(x\cdot(gh)=(x\cdot g)\cdot h\), and \(g\cdot x:=x\cdot g^{-1}\) turns it into a left one. The action is smooth if \(G\times X\to X\) is smooth, and linear if \(X\) is a vector space and each map \(x\mapsto g\cdot x\) is linear.

On weights, \(W\mapsto WR\) is a right action of \(O(n)\) on the input side and \(W\mapsto PW\) a left action of \(O(m)\) on the output side, and \((g,h)\cdot W=gWh^{-1}\) is a left action of \(GL_m\times GL_n\). On LoRA’s factors, \(g\cdot(B,A)=(Bg^{-1},gA)\) is a linear left action of \(GL_r\). A method is of action type when \(\rho(q)=\gamma(q)\cdot\theta_0\) for a map \(\gamma\) into a group acting on \(\Theta\), as for OFT and HRA. (This \(\gamma\) follows the exposés and is unrelated to the cotangent signal \(\gamma(t)\) of C.2 and D.26.) (IA)\(^3\) has the same form with the monoid \(\mathrm{Diag}_m\) in place of a group, and DoRA’s magnitude applies that monoid to LoRA’s weight.

Used in: Def I.2, Notation, Erlangen, Thm II.5, Thm III.5.

Definition D.13 (orbits, stabilisers, free actions). The orbit of \(x\) is \(G\cdot x=\{g\cdot x:g\in G\}\), its closure is the orbit closure, and its stabiliser is the closed subgroup \(G_x=\{g:g\cdot x=x\}\). For a smooth action of a Lie group, \(g\mapsto g\cdot x\) induces an injective immersion \(G/G_x\to X\) with image \(G\cdot x\), so the orbit is an immersed submanifold of dimension \(\dim G-\dim G_x\). The infinitesimal stabiliser \(\{\xi\in\mathfrak g:\xi\cdot x=0\}\) is the Lie algebra of \(G_x\). The action is free if every stabiliser is trivial, and the generic stabiliser is the stabiliser at a generic point, one in a suitable dense open set. Each map \(x\mapsto g\cdot x\) carries an orbit onto itself, so all its points look alike; orbits are homogeneous.

\(GL_r\) acts freely on pairs with \(\operatorname{rank}A=r\), since \(gA=A\) forces \(g=I\), so LoRA’s generic fibre, a single orbit, has dimension \(r^2\). LoHa’s joint gauge \(GL_{r_1}\times GL_{r_2}\times T_m\times T_n\) has a one-dimensional generic stabiliser, so its orbits have dimension \(r_1^2+r_2^2+m+n-1\). By homogeneity, the two-sided torus orbit \(\{\mathrm{diag}(a)W_0\mathrm{diag}(b)\}\) has dimension \(m+n-c(W_0)\) at each of its points, with \(c(W_0)\) the number of connected components of the support graph, the bipartite graph on the \(m\) rows and \(n\) columns with an edge \(\{i,j\}\) where \((W_0)_{ij}\ne0\). The images of (IA)\(^3\) and RoAd\(_1\) are orbit closures.

Used in: Erlangen, Thm II.5, Prop II.9, The image germ, Prop II.12, Rosetta entries 6 and 11. Reading: Lee (2013), Ch. 7 and 21.

Definition D.14 (Haar measure; invariant measures on homogeneous spaces). A compact Lie group \(K\) carries a unique invariant probability measure, its Haar measure. If \(K\) acts transitively on \(X\), so that \(X\cong K/K_x\) is a homogeneous space, then \(X\) carries a unique \(K\)-invariant probability measure, the image of Haar measure under \(k\mapsto k\cdot x\). The Grassmannian \(\mathrm{Gr}(r,n)\) of \(r\)-dimensional subspaces of \(\mathbb R^n\) (C.15) is a homogeneous space of \(O(n)\).

If \(A_0\in\mathbb R^{r\times n}\) has i.i.d. Gaussian entries, its law is unchanged by \(A_0\mapsto A_0R\) for \(R\in O(n)\), and \(A_0\) has rank \(r\) almost surely. So \(\mathrm{row}\,A_0\) is an \(O(n)\)-invariant random point of \(\mathrm{Gr}(r,n)\), and by uniqueness it is Haar-distributed. Vanilla LoRA with a Gaussian \(A_0\) therefore picks a Haar-random point of \(\mathrm{Gr}(r,n)\), the space that classifies its stratum \(\mathrm T_A\). Kaiming-uniform entries are not rotation invariant, so under HF’s default the point is not exactly Haar-distributed.

Fact D.16 (first fundamental theorem for \(O(n)\); Witt’s extension theorem). (First fundamental theorem.) Every polynomial in the rows \(w_1,\dots,w_m\in\mathbb R^n\) of \(W\) that is invariant under \(W\mapsto WR\), \(R\in O(n)\), is a polynomial in the inner products \(\langle w_i,w_j\rangle\), the entries of \(WW^\top\). Definition D.17 (Klein’s Erlangen programme). Klein (1872) proposed to define a geometry by a space together with a group acting on it, and to study the properties the group preserves. Euclidean geometry keeps lengths and angles, the invariants of rigid motions. Affine geometry keeps parallelism and ratios along parallel lines but not lengths or angles, because its group is larger. A larger group leaves fewer invariants.

For a group-type method the reading is a theorem, because the image lies in one orbit and so the method can change nothing the group preserves. For a cone such as LoRA’s image the word only names its covariance group, the largest \(\mathcal G_M\subseteq GL_m\times GL_n\) with \(\mathrm{Im}\,M(gW_0h^{-1})=g\,\mathrm{Im}\,M(W_0)\,h^{-1}\) (D.18). For LoRA this is all of \(GL_m\times GL_n\), for (IA)\(^3\) on keys and values it is \(\mathrm{Mon}_m\times GL_n\), and for the PiSSA split it is \(CO(m)\times CO(n)\).

Definition D.18 (equivariance and invariance of maps, update rules and merge rules). A map \(F:X\to Y\) between spaces with \(G\)-actions is equivariant if \(F(g\cdot x)=g\cdot F(x)\), and invariant if \(F(g\cdot x)=F(x)\). An update rule on parameters is equivariant under a subgroup \(H\) of the gauge if, started from \(h\cdot q\) with correspondingly transformed optimizer state, it produces \(h\) applied to the iterates started from \(q\). A rule that uses randomness is equivariant in distribution if \(F(g\cdot x)\) and \(g\cdot F(x)\) have the same law for every \(x\).

On LoRA’s full-rank locus, with channel-uniform hyperparameters, the largest such subgroup of \(GL_r\) is \(O(r)\) for SGD, \(B_r\) for Adam and all of \(GL_r\) for undamped scaled GD (D.23, Thm III.12). An equivariant rule started from gauge-related points traces the same weights, since \(\rho(h\cdot q)=\rho(q)\). Merge rules should be invariant under each adapter’s gauge. Task arithmetic \(\sum_iw_is_iB_iA_i\) is, while LoraHub’s \((\sum_iw_iB_i)(\sum_jw_jA_j)\) is not. On a weight space \(\mathbb R^N\), DARE’s random drop-and-rescale is equivariant under the invertible diagonal matrices \(T_N\) for each fixed mask, and under the monomial matrices \(\mathrm{Mon}_N\) (D.3) in distribution.

Definition D.19 (multihomogeneous maps; multidegree). Split the parameters into blocks \(q=(q_1,\dots,q_k)\). A map \(\delta\) is multihomogeneous of multidegree \((d_1,\dots,d_k)\) if \(\delta(c_1q_1,\dots,c_kq_k)=c_1^{d_1}\cdots c_k^{d_k}\,\delta(q)\) for all real \(c_i\). LoRA’s \(BA\) has multidegree \((1,1)\), and LoHa’s \((B_1A_1)\odot(B_2A_2)\) has multidegree \((1,1,1,1)\).

If some \(d_i\ge1\), the image of \(\delta\) contains \(0\) and is closed under multiplication by every nonnegative scalar, so it is a cone with vertex \(0\) (C.10). For a method \(\rho=\theta_b+s\,\delta\) started where \(\delta(q_0)=0\), so that \(\theta_b=\theta_0\), the image is the cone \(\theta_0+s\,\mathrm{Im}\,\delta\) with vertex \(\theta_0\). For \(c\in\mathbb R^k_{\gt0}\), the change \(q_i\mapsto c_iq_i\) together with \(s\mapsto s\prod_ic_i^{-d_i}\) leaves \(\rho\) unchanged. That rescaling is an isomorphism of methods and the source of the hyperparameter torus (D.20).

Definition D.20 (orbit space of a positive torus). Let \(\mathbb R^k_{\gt0}\) act on positive variables \(x\in\mathbb R^N_{\gt0}\) by \(x_j\mapsto x_j\prod_ic_i^{a_{ji}}\), with a real weight matrix \(a=(a_{ji})\). In logarithms this is translation by the column space of \(a\), so the orbits are parallel affine subspaces and the orbit space is a vector space of dimension \(N-\operatorname{rank}a\). The monomials \(\prod_jx_j^{b_j}\) with \(b^\top a=0\) are invariant, and a basis of such vectors \(b\) gives a complete set of invariants.

Under the hypotheses of Thm III.10, taken with Adam at \(\varepsilon=0\), no weight decay or clipping and a shared schedule (otherwise the per-block \(\varepsilon\), decay and clipping thresholds rescale as well), the torus acts on LoRA’s hyperparameters by \(s\mapsto s/(c_Ac_B)\), \(\eta_A\mapsto c_A\eta_A\), \(\eta_B\mapsto c_B\eta_B\), \(\sigma_A\mapsto c_A\sigma_A\), with \(\sigma_A\) the scale of \(A\)’s initialisation, without changing the weight trajectory. The invariants are \(s\eta_A\eta_B\) and \(\sigma_A/\eta_A\), so only two of the four knobs matter. LoRA+ with ratio \(\lambda\) replaces \(\eta_B\) by \(\lambda\eta_B\), which changes the invariants exactly as replacing \(\alpha\) by \(\lambda\alpha\) does. Under SGD the rates scale by \(c_i^2\), and the invariants become \(s^2\eta_A\eta_B\) and \(\sigma_A^2/\eta_A\).

Gauge, quotients and metrics

Definition D.21 (gauge symmetry of a parametrisation). The gauge group of a method \(\rho:Q\to\Theta\) is the group of diffeomorphisms \(\varphi\) of \(Q\) with \(\rho\circ\varphi=\rho\). If a Lie group \(G\) acts on \(Q\) with \(\rho(g\cdot q)=\rho(q)\), each orbit lies inside a fibre \(\rho^{-1}(\theta)\) (C.6), so \(d(M)\le\lvert M\rvert-\dim(\text{generic orbit})\). The action is locally transitive on a fibre when its orbits are open in the fibre, that is, when orbits and fibre have the same dimension there; on generic fibres this gives \(d(M)=\lvert M\rvert-\dim(\text{generic orbit})\), and the site calls such a \(G\) the generic gauge. Here the word means only this symmetry group; no connection is involved.

LoRA’s \(GL_r\) acts freely and locally transitively on full-rank fibres, so \(d=r(m+n)-r^2=r(m+n-r)\), and \(r^2\) parameters are gauge waste. HRA’s generic fibres have dimension \(r(r+1)/2\), while its \(r\) scalings \(u_i\mapsto c_iu_i\) sweep out only \(r\) of those dimensions, so the scalings do not fill the fibres; Thm II.7(a) describes the rest of the gauge as non-linear. For \(r=2\) the fibre through \((u_1,u_2)\) has dimension \(3\), made up of the two scalings and of rotating \(u_1\) and \(u_2\) by a common angle inside their plane, which keeps the angle between them and hence \(H_{u_1}H_{u_2}\), the rotation of that plane by twice the angle. The plane depends on the point, so this move is not a linear action on parameters. The Hurwitz moves \((u_i,u_{i+1})\mapsto(H_{u_i}u_{i+1},u_i)\) preserve the product of reflections, since \(H_aH_b=H_{H_ab}H_a\), and they satisfy the braid relations, so the braid group on \(r\) strands, unrelated to the signed permutations \(B_r\), acts on HRA’s parameters. These moves are discrete and add no dimension to the fibre.

Fact D.22 (quotients and descent; the quotient manifold theorem). For an action of \(G\) on \(X\), the orbit space \(X/G\) is the set of orbits, with the map \(\pi:x\mapsto G\cdot x\). A map \(f:X\to Y\) that is constant on orbits factors uniquely as \(f=\bar f\circ\pi\); this is descent. (Quotient manifold theorem.) If a Lie group acts smoothly, freely and properly on a manifold \(X\), then \(X/G\) is a manifold of dimension \(\dim X-\dim G\), \(\pi\) is a submersion, and \(\bar f\) is smooth whenever \(f\) is. Properness means that \((g,x)\mapsto(g\cdot x,x)\) is a proper map, one under which preimages of compact sets are compact. (Lee, 2013, Thm 21.10.)

\(GL_r\) acts freely and properly on pairs \((B,A)\) of full rank, and \((B,A)\mapsto BA\) identifies the quotient with \(\mathcal M_r\), of dimension \(r(m+n)-r^2\). A rule for combining LoRA factors is a function of the adapters exactly when it is constant on the fibres of \((B_i,A_i)\mapsto(B_iA_i)\), and a cometric on factors that depends only on \(W\) descends to \(\mathcal M_r\).

Used in: Prop V.4, Prop III.4, Obs II.15, When a change of chart does not matter.

Definition D.23 (metrics, cometrics and the pulled-back cometric). A metric on a vector space \(V\) is a positive-definite inner product, equivalently a symmetric positive-definite map \(g:V\to V^*\), that is, \(\langle gv,v\rangle\gt0\) for \(v\ne0\). A cometric is a symmetric positive semidefinite map \(K:V^*\to V\). It turns a covector, such as a gradient \(\nabla_\theta L\), into a velocity \(-K\nabla_\theta L\). A metric gives the cometric \(K=g^{-1}\), while a cometric may be degenerate. Metrics pull back along immersions, and cometrics push forward along any smooth map. For instance, gradient flow \(\dot q=-\Lambda\nabla_q(L\circ\rho)\) moves the weights by \(\dot\theta=-K_q\nabla_\theta L\), with \[K_q=D\rho_q\,\Lambda\,D\rho_q^\top.\]

For LoRA with block-scalar rates, one rate per factor, \(\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\eta_BI,\eta_AI)\) on \((B,A)\) and \(K(G)=s^2(\eta_BGA^\top A+\eta_ABB^\top G)\). Two points of one fibre give the same weight velocity for every loss exactly when their cometrics agree. At points where \(A\) has full row rank and \(r\lt n\), this holds for LoRA’s linear gauge and plain gradient descent exactly along \(O(r)\). A rule descends to a cometric on weights when it gives the same weight velocity at all points of each fibre; with a point-dependent preconditioner in place of \(\Lambda\), undamped scaled GD and the SGD variant of LoRA-Pro do, on the full-rank locus. Scaled GD multiplies the \(B\)-gradient by \((AA^\top)^{-1}\) and the \(A\)-gradient by \((B^\top B)^{-1}\), and undamped means that no ridge term \(\delta I\) is added before inverting (Prop III.4).

Used in: Def III.1, Obs III.2, Thm III.3, Prop III.4, The metric a method carries, Three invariants.

Definition D.24 (Riemannian gradient flow; tangent projection; retraction). On a submanifold \(N\subseteq\Theta\) with the metric induced by the Frobenius inner product, the Riemannian gradient of \(L|_N\) at \(\theta\) is the orthogonal projection \(\Pi_{T_\theta N}\nabla L(\theta)\) of the ambient gradient onto the tangent space, and the Riemannian gradient flow is \(\dot\theta=-\Pi_{T_\theta N}\nabla L(\theta)\). A discrete step along the tangent space leaves \(N\), so it is followed by a retraction, a smooth map \(R_\theta\) from a neighbourhood of \(0\) in \(T_\theta N\) to \(N\) with \(R_\theta(0)=\theta\) and \(DR_\theta(0)=\mathrm{id}\).

At \(W=W_0+X\) with \(X\in\mathcal M_r\), \(U=\mathrm{col}\,X\) and \(V=\mathrm{row}\,X\), the tangent space is \(\{D:P_{U^\perp}DP_{V^\perp}=0\}\) (C.12) and the projection is \(P_UG+GP_V-P_UGP_V\). This is LoRA-Pro’s equivalent gradient, so the SGD variant of LoRA-Pro follows the Riemannian gradient flow on \(W_0+\mathcal M_r\). Truncating the SVD of \(X+\xi\) to rank \(r\) is a retraction onto \(\mathcal M_r\), the one Riemannion uses.

Used in: Prop III.4, When a change of chart does not matter, Thm III.12. Reading: Absil, Mahony and Sepulchre, Optimization Algorithms on Matrix Manifolds (2008), Ch. 3 and 4.

Fact D.25 (distributions, Lie brackets and Frobenius’ theorem). A distribution \(D\) of rank \(k\) on an open set \(U\) assigns to each point \(\theta\) a \(k\)-dimensional subspace \(D_\theta\) of directions, smoothly in \(\theta\); the word is unrelated to probability distributions. The Lie bracket of vector fields is \([X,Y]=DY\cdot X-DX\cdot Y\); flowing along \(X\), \(Y\), \(-X\), \(-Y\) for time \(t\) misses the starting point by \(t^2[X,Y]+O(t^3)\). \(D\) is involutive if \([X,Y]\) lies in \(D\) whenever \(X\) and \(Y\) do. (Frobenius.) \(D\) is involutive iff every point lies on a \(k\)-dimensional integral manifold, a submanifold whose tangent space at each of its points \(\theta\) is \(D_\theta\). A maximal connected integral manifold is a leaf. (Lee, 2013, Ch. 19.) The Frobenius algebras of B.6 and the Frobenius norm share only the name.

On \(\mathbb R^3\), \(D=\mathrm{span}\{\partial_x,\ \partial_y+x\,\partial_z\}\) is not involutive, since \([\partial_x,\partial_y+x\,\partial_z]=\partial_z\notin D\), so no surface is tangent to \(D\). A backward method with anchor \(a(\theta)\) chooses \(D_\theta=\mathrm{im}\,a(\theta)\), and it reads as a forward method only along integral manifolds. GaLore with a projector fixed on each period, LISA and MeZO have constant distributions, which are involutive with affine leaves. Full-batch GaLore with \(T=1\), read as \(D_W=\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb R^n,U_r(\nabla L(W)))\) wherever a singular-value gap exists, already fails to be involutive for \(L=\tfrac12\|W\|_F^2\).

Used in: When a backward method is a forward one, Prop IV.3.

Conservation and balance

Fact D.26 (Noether’s theorem for gradient flows). Let a Lie group act linearly on \(Q=\mathbb R^N\) with \(\rho(k\cdot q)=\rho(q)\), let \(\Lambda\succ0\) be a constant rate matrix, and let \(\gamma(t)\) be any time-dependent covector on \(\Theta\), a gradient or not. Along every solution of \(\dot q=-\Lambda\,D\rho_q^\top\gamma(t)\), and for every generator \(\xi\) of the action with \(\Lambda^{-1}\xi\) symmetric, the charge \[J_\xi(q)=\tfrac12\langle q,\Lambda^{-1}\xi\,q\rangle\] is constant. (Kunin et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2023; Marcotte, Gribonval and Peyré, 2023.)
More: the one-line proof

Invariance of \(\rho\) gives \(D\rho_q(\xi q)=0\). Hence \(\tfrac{d}{dt}J_\xi=\langle\Lambda^{-1}\xi q,\dot q\rangle=-\langle\xi q,D\rho_q^\top\gamma\rangle=-\langle D\rho_q\,\xi q,\gamma\rangle=0\).

For scalar LoRA \(w=w_0+ba\) with rates \(\eta_B,\eta_A\), the charge \(b^2/\eta_B-a^2/\eta_A\) is conserved, so every trajectory runs along a hyperbola, the Noether hyperbolas of Figure 8. Noether (1918) derived conservation laws from symmetries of a Lagrangian; this version needs no Lagrangian and holds for any loss and data. For LoRA with rates \(\eta_B,\eta_A\), the generators \(\xi_X(B,A)=(BX,-XA)\) pass the test exactly for symmetric \(X\), and the \(r(r+1)/2\) charges are the entries of \(\Phi=B^\top B/\eta_B-AA^\top/\eta_A\). Minibatches, LoRA dropout, global-norm clipping and DoRA’s detached norm keep the required form. Momentum and Adam do not, and under discrete SGD the charge drifts, by an amount relative to the charge of order \(\eta^2\) per step.

Used in: Thm III.5, Isometries and charges, Noether; gauge, Figure 8, Things you can check, Prediction X.8, Seven borrowed words.

Definition D.27 (Cartan decomposition; polar decomposition). The map \(\vartheta(\xi)=-\xi^\top\) is an automorphism of \(\mathfrak{gl}_r\) of order two, a Cartan involution. Its \(+1\) and \(-1\) eigenspaces give the Cartan decomposition \(\mathfrak{gl}_r=\mathfrak{so}(r)\oplus\mathrm{Sym}_r\), \(\xi=\tfrac12(\xi-\xi^\top)+\tfrac12(\xi+\xi^\top)\), with \([\mathfrak{so},\mathfrak{so}]\subseteq\mathfrak{so}\), \([\mathfrak{so},\mathrm{Sym}]\subseteq\mathrm{Sym}\) and \([\mathrm{Sym},\mathrm{Sym}]\subseteq\mathfrak{so}\). Its group version is the polar decomposition, which says that \((k,S)\mapsto k\,e^{S}\) is a diffeomorphism \(O(r)\times\mathrm{Sym}_r\to GL_r\). Hence \(g\mapsto g^\top g\) identifies the cosets \(O(r)g\) with the positive-definite matrices, \(O(r)\backslash GL_r\cong\mathrm{Sym}^+_r\).

With block-scalar rates (D.23), the antisymmetric generators \(X\in\mathfrak{so}(r)\) of LoRA’s gauge act by rotations \((BO^\top,OA)\) that preserve the rate metric, so the optimizer cannot feel them; they are isometries. The symmetric generators \(X\in\mathrm{Sym}_r\) give the conserved charges of D.26. For general rates the split needs \(\mathfrak g\) to be closed under \(\xi\mapsto\Lambda\xi^\top\Lambda^{-1}\). Gradient descent on LoRA is thus a family of dynamics indexed by \(O(r)\backslash GL_r\), and the Kempf–Ness argument needs only curves \(e^{t\xi}\) with \(\xi\) symmetric.

Used in: Thm III.5, Isometries and charges, Three invariants, The thesis, Prop III.7, When a change of chart does not matter. Reading: Knapp, Lie Groups Beyond an Introduction (2002), §VI.2; Horn and Johnson, Matrix Analysis (2013), §7.3.

Definition D.28 (balancedness; the conserved \(B^\top B-AA^\top\)). A factorisation \(W=BA\) is balanced if \(B^\top B=AA^\top\), and rate-weighted balanced if \(\Phi=B^\top B/\eta_B-AA^\top/\eta_A=0\), that is, \(B^\top B=cAA^\top\) with \(c=\eta_B/\eta_A\). For a deep linear network \(W=W_L\cdots W_1\) the same word means \(W_{j+1}^\top W_{j+1}=W_jW_j^\top\) for every \(j\).

Under gradient flow with block-scalar rates, \(\Phi\) is conserved (D.26), so rate-weighted balancedness, once it holds, holds forever; at equal rates the two notions agree. Zero-product starts \(B_0A_0=0\) have \(\langle B_0^\top B_0,A_0A_0^\top\rangle_F=0\), which makes them maximally unbalanced for given norms of the two Gram matrices, since the inner product of two positive semidefinite matrices is never negative. From \(B_0=0\), conservation gives \(B^\top B=\tfrac{\eta_B}{\eta_A}(AA^\top-A_0A_0^\top)\), so \(AA^\top\) never drops below \(A_0A_0^\top\) in the Loewner order. HF’s PiSSA starts with \(B_0^\top B_0=A_0A_0^\top\). When \(\Phi\) is isotropic, singular values of \(\Delta W\) well above the crossover scale \(\sigma^*\) of Thm III.9, set by the charge, the scale and the rates, follow the balanced dynamics of Arora, Cohen and Hazan.

Used in: Isometries and charges, Prop III.7, Prop III.8, Thm III.9, References. Reading: Arora, Cohen and Hazan (ICML 2018); Du, Hu and Lee (NeurIPS 2018).

Definition D.29 (the moment map). Let a group \(G\subseteq GL(V)\) that is closed under transpose act on a real inner-product space \(V\), and split \(\mathfrak g=\mathfrak k\oplus\mathfrak p\) into its antisymmetric and symmetric elements. The (real) moment map \(\mu:V\to\mathfrak p\) is defined by \[\langle\mu(v),\xi\rangle=\tfrac12\tfrac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}\|e^{t\xi}v\|^2=\langle\xi v,v\rangle\qquad(\xi\in\mathfrak p).\] It records how the norm changes along the symmetric directions of the orbit; the antisymmetric directions preserve the norm.

For LoRA’s gauge, \(\tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_{0}\big(\|Be^{-tX}\|^2+\|e^{tX}A\|^2\big)=-2\operatorname{tr}(X\mu)\) for symmetric \(X\), with \(\mu=B^\top B-AA^\top\), so this \(\mu\) is the moment map up to sign. Its zero set is the balanced slice, and at equal rates \(\eta_A=\eta_B=\eta\) it equals \(\eta\Phi\), a multiple of the Noether charge.

Used in: The balanced slice, Prop III.7, Cor III.6, Figure 8.

Fact D.30 (Kempf–Ness theorem, real form). Let \(G\subseteq GL(V)\) be a real algebraic group, cut out by polynomial equations, that is closed under transpose (such a group is reductive), for instance \(GL_r\) acting on LoRA factors, and let \(K=G\cap O(V)\). For \(v\in V\), (i) \(g=e\) is a critical point of \(g\mapsto\|g\cdot v\|^2\) iff \(\mu(v)=0\), and then \(v\) has least norm on \(G\cdot v\); (ii) \(G\cdot v\) is closed iff it contains a point with \(\mu=0\); (iii) on a closed orbit, the points with \(\mu=0\) form a single \(K\)-orbit; (iv) the closure of every orbit contains exactly one closed orbit. (Kempf and Ness, 1979; Birkes, 1971; Richardson and Slodowy, 1990.)
More: idea of the proof

For symmetric \(\xi\), \(\|e^{t\xi}v\|^2=\sum_ie^{2t\lambda_i}\|v_i\|^2\) in an eigenbasis of \(\xi\), which is convex in \(t\). By the polar decomposition every orbit point is \(k\,e^{\xi}v\) with \(k\in K\) norm-preserving, so critical points are minima and two minima differ by an element of \(K\).

For \(m=n=r=1\), \(t\in\mathbb R^\times\) acts by \(t\cdot(b,a)=(b/t,ta)\) and \(\mu=b^2-a^2\). The orbits are the hyperbolas \(ba=c\ne0\), which are closed and meet \(\mu=0\) in the two points \(\pm(b,a)\) with \(\lvert b\rvert=\lvert a\rvert\), one \(O(1)\)-orbit; the two punctured axes, which are not closed and avoid \(\mu=0\); and the origin. For LoRA in general, \(K=O(r)\), and a full-rank orbit is closed, being a whole fibre of \((B,A)\mapsto BA\). The least-norm factorisations of a fixed \(BA\) are the balanced ones, they form one \(O(r)\)-orbit, and there \(\|B\|_F^2+\|A\|_F^2=2\|BA\|_*\). Weight decay adds a norm to the loss (Cor III.6). With learning-rate-coupled decay, which multiplies the decay of each factor by its rate, \(\dot\Phi=-2\lambda\mu\), so the equilibria lie on \(\mu=0\), and \(\mu\) decays exponentially at equal rates. With per-time decay, the same \(\lambda\) for both factors, \(\dot\Phi=-2\lambda\Phi\), so \(\Phi\to0\).

Used in: The balanced slice, Prop III.7, Cor III.6, Combining, Known results, Atlas.

Fact D.31 (the closed orbit in each fibre of \((B,A)\mapsto BA\)). For \(GL_r\) acting on factor pairs, fix \(W\) of rank \(k\le r\). Among the pairs with \(BA=W\), those with \(\operatorname{rank}B=\operatorname{rank}A=k\) form a single orbit. It is closed, and it lies in the closure of every orbit in the fibre \(\{BA=W\}\). Consequently a continuous \(GL_r\)-invariant function is constant on each fibre of \((B,A)\mapsto BA\). For a complex reductive group acting on a vector space, every fibre of the quotient map likewise contains exactly one closed orbit (Mumford, Fogarty and Kirwan, Geometric Invariant Theory, 1994, Ch. 1).

For \(m=n=r=1\) (D.30), the fibre \(\{ba=0\}\) consists of the origin and the two punctured axes, and the origin, its only closed orbit, lies in the closure of both axes. This is the step behind Prop V.4(a). A continuous rule for combining LoRA factors that is invariant under each adapter’s \(GL_r\) is automatically a function of the adapters \(B_iA_i\), also off the full-rank locus. Continuity matters. The projector onto \(\mathrm{row}\,A\), defined where \(\operatorname{rank}A=r\), is invariant, yet it differs at \((0,A)\) and \((0,A')\), which have the same product \(0\).

Used in: Prop V.4, Combining.

Matrix and convexity tools

Fact D.32 (Ky Fan’s maximum principle). Let \(S\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}\) be symmetric with eigenvalues \(\lambda_1\ge\dots\ge\lambda_n\), and let \(1\le k\le n\). Then \[\max\{\operatorname{tr}(PSP^\top):P\in\mathbb R^{k\times n},\ PP^\top=I_k\}=\lambda_1+\dots+\lambda_k,\] attained when the rows of \(P\) span a top-\(k\) eigenspace. For any matrix \(X\) with singular values \(\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\cdots\), applying this to \(S=X^\top X\) gives \(\max_P\|XP^\top\|_F^2=\sigma_1^2+\dots+\sigma_k^2\), and for square \(X\) also \(\|PXP^\top\|_F^2\le\|XP^\top\|_F^2\le\sigma_1^2+\dots+\sigma_k^2\). (K. Fan, PNAS 35, 1949; Bhatia, Matrix Analysis, 1997, Ch. III.)

Among \(A_0\) with \(A_0A_0^\top=cI\), LoRA’s initial rate \(\|GA_0^\top\|_F^2\) under gradient flow is largest when \(\mathrm{row}\,A_0\) is the top-\(r\) right singular space of \(G\), which is LoRA-GA’s choice for \(A_0\). For LOFT, the squared norm of the pulled-back gradient \(P_r\operatorname{skew}(W_0^\top G)P_r^\top\) is at most \(2\sum_{k\le\lfloor r/2\rfloor}\mu_k^2\), where \(\pm i\mu_k\) are the eigenvalues of the skew matrix \(\operatorname{skew}(W_0^\top G)\), whose singular values are \(\mu_1,\mu_1,\mu_2,\mu_2,\dots\). The floor appears because a skew matrix compressed to an odd-dimensional subspace has a zero eigenvalue, and Cauchy interlacing bounds the rest.

Used in: LOFT, Prop II.3.

Fact D.33 (Sylvester equations). For \(S\in\mathbb R^{p\times p}\), \(T\in\mathbb R^{q\times q}\) and \(C\in\mathbb R^{p\times q}\), the Sylvester equation \(SX+XT=C\) has a unique solution \(X\) for every \(C\) iff \(S\) and \(-T\) have no eigenvalue in common, since the eigenvalues of \(X\mapsto SX+XT\) are the sums \(\lambda_i(S)+\lambda_j(T)\). If \(S\) and \(T\) are symmetric positive definite, this holds and \(X=\int_0^\infty e^{-tS}Ce^{-tT}\,dt\). The Bartels–Stewart algorithm solves the equation through Schur forms of \(S\) and \(T\). (Horn and Johnson, 1991, Ch. 4.)

LoRA-Pro’s equivalent gradient leaves a free \(r\times r\) matrix \(X\). The published method picks it by solving \(B^\top B\,X+X\,AA^\top=C\), with \(C\) built from the gradient. On the full-rank locus both Gram matrices are positive definite, so \(X\) exists and is unique. The first-order weight velocity does not depend on \(X\), while the resulting parameter update is only \(O(r)\)-equivariant.

Used in: Thm III.12, When a change of chart does not matter, Figure 9.

Definition D.34 (the perspective map; convex combinations). The perspective map is \(\pi(Z,S)=S/Z\) on \(\{(Z,S)\in\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^d:Z\gt0\}\). It is constant on rays, \(\pi(tZ,tS)=\pi(Z,S)\) for \(t\gt0\), and it turns sums into convex combinations, \[\pi(Z_1+Z_2,S_1+S_2)=\lambda\,\pi(Z_1,S_1)+(1-\lambda)\,\pi(Z_2,S_2),\qquad\lambda=\frac{Z_1}{Z_1+Z_2}.\]

For a fixed query \(q\), attention is \(\mathrm{Attn}_q(K)=\pi(\Sigma_q(K))\), where \(\Sigma_q(K)=\big(\sum e^{\langle q,k\rangle/\sqrt d},\ \sum e^{\langle q,k\rangle/\sqrt d}\,v\big)\) is a monoid homomorphism on multisets of key–value pairs (D.10). With a prefix \(P\) before the content \(C\), the identity above gives \(\mathrm{Attn}_q(P\uplus C)=(1-\lambda_q)\mathrm{Attn}_q(C)+\lambda_q\mathrm{Attn}_q(P)\), with \(\mathrm{Attn}_q(P)\) in the convex hull \(\mathrm{conv}\,V_P\) of the prefix values, so a prefix acts as a parallel branch gated by its share of the mass.

Used in: Thm VI.5, Attention, Figure 13. Reading: Boyd and Vandenberghe, Convex Optimization (2004), §2.3.3 and §3.2.6.
