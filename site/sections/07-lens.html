Exposé IV

The lens: tangent and cotangent methods

Backpropagation pairs every map with its reverse derivative, so a method can shape what the model expresses on the forward side or compress the gradient on the backward side. For linear methods and optimizers that read only their gradients, the two sides give the same weights, and GaLore becomes one-sided LoRA with a moving frame and carried optimizer state.

Every arrow can be read twice. Forward, it says where the point may go. Backward, it says what the point is allowed to hear.

a working maxim
In one minute
  • Backpropagation pairs every map with its reverse derivative, a lens. A method can change the forward map, and so what the model can express (LoRA), or only compress the gradient on the way back (GaLore, LISA, MeZO).
  • If an adapter is linear in its trainable tensor and the optimizer reads only its gradients (Adam with no weight decay and no global-norm clipping, say), the two readings give identical weights. GaLore is then one-sided LoRA with its projector as frozen factor, re-based every \(T\) steps with the optimizer state carried over (Torroba-Hennigen et al., 2025).
  • Frobenius’ theorem decides when a backward method’s directions could come from forward methods. It is vacuous for GaLore, LISA and MeZO, which fix their directions for a period, and full-batch GaLore with \(T=1\) can fail it at every rank.
  • Only the backprop cone costs activation memory: inputs of trainable linear boxes and residuals of nonlinear boxes downstream of them. Frozen linear layers need to keep nothing, so LoRA-FA and ladder side-tuning save activations, while LISA and prompt tuning truncate nothing.

Two sides of one port

A layer does two jobs in training. Forward, it turns parameters and inputs into outputs; backward, it turns a cotangent at the output into cotangents at the input and the parameters. Backpropagation keeps the pair together, and a category theorist calls it a lens.

CreditThe equivalence between GaLore and one-sided LoRA is due to Torroba-Hennigen, Lang, Guo and Kim, 2025, who prove a duality between linear gradient transformations and adapters (their Thm 1 and Cor. 3, with weight decay in App. B). The frozen-\(A\) case under SGD, with random projections, appears earlier in Hao, Cao and Mou, 2024. Our additions are the lens bookkeeping, the explicit hypotheses on the optimizer, and the table that sorts the cotangent family.

Every method in Exposés I to III changes the forward job, replacing the parameter port \(\Theta\) by a smaller space \(Q\) and a map \(\rho:Q\to\Theta\). A second family edits only the backward job. GaLore compresses the weight gradient \(G\) to \(P^\top G\), with \(P\) from an SVD of \(G\), runs Adam on that small matrix and maps the result back with \(P\). Every weight trains; only the optimizer state is small. LISA and MeZO follow the same pattern.

When \(\rho\) is linear the two families meet. The pullback of the forward method is then the compression of a backward method, and an optimizer that reads only its gradients cannot tell the two apart. Figure 10 sorts the backward family and draws what backpropagation must store.

Backpropagation as a lens

In the language of Def I.1, a layer is a parametrised map \(f:P\times X\to Y\). Its reverse derivative \(\mathsf R[f](x,y)=J_f(x)^\top y\) takes an output cotangent to an input cotangent, parameters included, and backpropagation is the 2-functor

\[\mathbf{Para}(\mathsf R):\mathbf{Para}(\mathcal C)\longrightarrow\mathbf{Para}(\mathbf{Lens}(\mathcal C)),\qquad f\longmapsto(f,\mathsf R[f]).\]

A reparametrisation \(\rho\) is a 2-cell, so the functor turns it into a lens \((\rho,\mathsf R[\rho])\). Its tangent side is the image, what the method can express (Exposé II). Its cotangent side \(\mathsf R[\rho](q,\gamma)=D\rho_q^\top\gamma\) pulls the weight gradient back to \(Q\) and, with the rates \(\Lambda\), induces the cometric \(K=D\rho\,\Lambda\,D\rho^\top\) (Def III.1). A lens need not come from a map, though, and a backward half written down directly gives the second family.

CreditThe parametrised-map construction and the reading of backpropagation as a functor into lenses are due to Fong, Spivak and Tuyéras, 2017 and Cruttwell, Gavranović, Ghani, Wilson and Zanasi, 2021.
Definition IV.1 (forward and backward methods). A forward method acts on the parameter port by a lens in the image of \(\mathsf R\), namely \((\rho,\mathsf R[\rho])\). A backward, or cotangent-side, method acts by a lens whose forward part is \(\mathrm{id}_\Theta\) and whose backward part is a compression \(c_t:T^*_\theta\Theta\to S\) into a state space \(S\), paired with an anchor \(a_t:S\to T_\theta\Theta\). The update is \[\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\,a_t\big(\mathrm{opt}(c_t(\nabla L))\big).\]

More: why Flora falls outside

Flora compresses its momentum or gradient accumulator with a random projection and hands the decompressed tensor to Adafactor, which acts in the full space, so its update need not lie in the image of the anchor. On resampling it transports its momentum to the new frame. It fits Def IV.1 only in an idealised form with an SGD or heavy-ball base. An earlier version of the table above listed Flora where GaLore with a random projector now stands.

The degree-one collapse

If \(\rho(C)=W_k+\mathcal LC\), training \(C\) and the backward method with \(c=\mathcal L^*\), \(a=\mathcal L\) feed the optimizer the same gradients \(\mathcal L^*G\) and so make the same weight updates. A curved \(\rho\), such as LoRA’s \(BA\), separates them after one step, and so does an optimizer that reads the parameter tensor, \(C\) in one procedure and \(W\) in the other.

Proposition IV.2 (the degree-one collapse). Known Let \(\rho_k(C)=W_k+\mathcal LC\) with \(\mathcal L\) linear and \(C_0=0\). Let opt be a first-order optimizer whose state and output depend only on the sequence of gradients it is fed, and which does not read the parameter tensor: SGD, heavy-ball or Nesterov momentum, Adam or AMSGrad, or Adafactor with scale_parameter=False (fixed or relative step, factorising the compressed gradient in the shape of \(C\)), each with no weight decay, coupled or decoupled. Run both procedures with the same implementation of opt, on the same minibatches and from the same optimizer state, fresh or carried over. Then they produce the same \(W\)-trajectory over a period:
  • training \(C\) with opt and merging at the end of the period;
  • the backward method with \(c=\mathcal L^*\) and \(a=\mathcal L\), started at \(W_k\).

AdamW with \(\lambda\gt0\), LAMB and LARS trust ratios, parameter-scaled Adafactor (scale_parameter=True, the default in HF and in galore_torch’s GaLoreAdafactor) and global-norm clipping of the full gradient, such as HF Trainer’s default max_grad_norm=1.0 whenever it triggers, all break the identity. Injectivity of \(\mathcal L\) is not needed.

In particular, on each matrix GaLore projects (its other parameters train in full in both procedures), GaLore with a projector \(P_k\) fixed on each period equals one-sided LoRA \(W_k+P_kC\) (frozen \(B=P_k\), or frozen \(A\) when projecting on the right), merged and re-based every period. This needs the hypotheses above, \(C\)’s optimizer state (moments and step counter) carried over unchanged rather than reset, and \(P_k\) equal to GaLore’s matrix itself, signs and column order included; its span alone does not suffice. ReLoRA-style resets give a different trajectory. GaLore’s scale \(\alpha\) multiplies the optimizer output, so it is a learning-rate factor.

By induction, \(W_t=W_k+\mathcal LC_t\) along the first procedure. Both procedures therefore feed opt the same sequence \(\mathcal L^*\nabla L(W_t)\) from the same state, and they receive the same outputs \(U_t\). One sets \(C\leftarrow C-\eta U_t\), which moves the weight by \(-\eta\mathcal LU_t\); the other sets \(W\leftarrow W-\eta\mathcal LU_t\) directly. Injectivity of \(\mathcal L\) is never used.

The excluded ingredients see different tensors in the two procedures. Decoupled decay acts on \(C\) in one, pulling \(W\) toward \(W_k\), and on \(W\) in the other, pulling it toward \(0\); coupled decay adds \(\lambda C\) to the gradient in one and \(\lambda W\) in the other. Parameter-scaled steps read \(\mathrm{RMS}(C)\) in one and \(\mathrm{RMS}(W)\) in the other. Full-gradient clipping uses \(\|G\|\) in one and \(\|\mathcal L^*G\|\) in the other.

For GaLore, apply this on each period with \(\mathcal LC=P_kC\) (or \(\mathcal LC=CP_k^\top\) when projecting on the right). GaLore’s update is \(W\leftarrow W-\eta\,\alpha\,P_k\,\mathrm{opt}(P_k^\top G)\), so \(\alpha\) multiplies the learning rate. Since the trajectories agree up to the start of a period, so do the gradients there, and both procedures read off the same \(P_k\). The state handed from one period to the next is the same object in both procedures, so the induction runs across periods. That state holds coordinates relative to \(P_{k-1}\)’s columns, so replacing \(P_k\) by another basis of its span changes what the reused moments mean.

Beyond one-sided LoRA, GaLore adds a frame read off the gradient’s SVD and the carry-over of optimizer state. The carry-over reads moments written in the coordinates of \(P_{k-1}\) as coordinates for \(P_k\), with no change of basis. LDAdam and Flora transport the state instead.

More: scale, implementation, review

Moving GaLore’s \(\alpha\) inside \(\rho\) as a LoRA scale multiplies the compressed gradient by \(\alpha\). Under Adam this gives GaLore’s trajectory only at \(\varepsilon=0\), or with \(\varepsilon\) multiplied by \(\alpha\) as well. This is the exchange of scale for learning rate and \(\varepsilon\) on the hyperparameter torus of Thm III.10.

Both sides must run the same implementation. galore_torch’s AdamW adds \(\varepsilon\) to \(\sqrt v\) before the bias correction, as in transformers, and swapping in torch.optim.Adam on the LoRA side moves the trajectory by about \(4\times10^{-5}\) in the galore_torch check quoted in the proof.

When a backward method is a forward one

A backward method chooses at each weight a space of directions \(D_\theta=\mathrm{im}\,a(\theta)\), and it reads as a forward method only if these fit into \(k\)-dimensional surfaces, the images of immersive parametrisations. Frobenius’ theorem tests this. The bracket \([X,Y]\), the gap by which a small loop along \(X\), \(Y\), \(-X\), \(-Y\) fails to close, must stay in \(D\). Discrete steps and the optimizer’s metric ask for more.

Proposition IV.3 (integrability). Frobenius Let \(D\) be a smooth distribution of constant rank \(k\) on an open set \(U\subseteq\Theta\), for instance the anchor distribution \(D_\theta=\mathrm{im}\,a(\theta)\) of a time-independent backward method. Then \(D\) is involutive on \(U\) iff through every \(\theta\in U\) passes an injective immersion \(\rho_\theta:(\mathbb R^k,0)\to(U,\theta)\) with \(\mathrm{im}\,D\rho_\theta=D\) along its image. A single such \(\rho\) through \(\theta_0\) gives involutivity only along \(\mathrm{im}\,\rho\). For example, \(D=\mathrm{span}\{\partial_x,\ \partial_y+xz\,\partial_z\}\) on \(\mathbb R^3\) has \([\partial_x,\partial_y+xz\,\partial_z]=z\,\partial_z\notin D\) off \(z=0\), yet the plane \(z=0\) is an integral manifold.

The dynamic content needs separate hypotheses.

  • In continuous time, if \(D\) is involutive, a backward flow with \(\dot\theta\in D_\theta\) stays on the leaf through \(\theta_0\).
  • In the discrete update of Def IV.1, the iterates stay on a leaf for all step sizes and optimizer outputs iff the leaf contains \((\theta+D_\theta)\cap U\) at every iterate, that is, iff the leaves are affine, as with an anchor constant over the period (Prop IV.2).
  • Under gradient flow, equivalence with a forward lens \((\rho,\mathsf R[\rho])\) also needs matching frames, \(a_tc_t=D\rho\,\Lambda\,D\rho^\top\) along the trajectory, so \(K=ac\) is symmetric PSD and, for injective \(a\), \(c=Ma^\top\) with \(M\) symmetric PSD on \(S\). If \(\rho\) is an immersion with \(\dim Q=k\), the leaf metric induced by \(K\) must also be flat; methods with gauge fibres escape this. Exact agreement of discrete steps for every step size further forces \(\rho\) to be affine along each line \(q+t\,\Lambda D\rho_q^\top g\).

Instances. GaLore with a projector fixed on each period, its random-projector variant, LISA and MeZO have anchors that do not depend on \(\theta\) within a period, so their distributions are constant and trivially involutive with affine leaves. Under the hypotheses of Prop IV.2 (which LISA with AdamW at positive decay violates) they are rebasing schemes of linear forward methods, whose reach Thm V.3 describes. At \(T=1\) this reading is automatic; each MeZO step is, up to its finite-difference estimator, a step of \(\theta_t+\lambda z_t\). What separates these methods from LoRA is the schedule and the carried state, and integrability plays no part in it.

Non-involutivity needs a \(\theta\)-dependent anchor. Full-batch GaLore with \(T=1\), read as \(D_W=\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb R^n,U_r(\nabla L(W)))\) where a singular-value gap exists (for the right projection, \(\mathrm{Hom}(V_r(\nabla L(W)),\mathbb R^m)\)), need not be involutive. For every \(r\) with \(1\le r\lt\min(m,n)\) it fails for \(L=\tfrac12\|W\|_F^2\), and numerically for least squares and cross-entropy with several samples; with one least-squares sample it is involutive. Minibatch GaLore with \(T=1\) and MeZO are not time-independent, since their anchors depend on step-wise randomness.

The first equivalence is Frobenius’ theorem. The tangent spaces of the image of an injective immersion are involutive along that image, which gives the remark about a single \(\rho\), and the bracket in the example is a direct computation. For the discrete statement, \(\theta-\eta\,a(\theta)s\) stays on the leaf for all \(\eta\) and \(s\) iff the leaf contains \((\theta+D_\theta)\cap U\).

For the frame condition, gradient flow of the forward method moves the weight by \(-D\rho\,\Lambda\,D\rho^\top\nabla_\theta L\) (Def III.1), and agreement for every loss forces \(a_tc_t=D\rho\,\Lambda\,D\rho^\top\). This operator is symmetric PSD with range in \(\mathrm{im}\,a\), which gives \(c=Ma^\top\) when \(a\) is injective. When \(\rho\) is an immersion with \(\dim Q=k\), it is a local diffeomorphism onto the leaf, and \(D\rho\) carries the flat metric \(\Lambda^{-1}\) on \(Q\) to the metric \(K^{-1}\) on the leaf, which is therefore flat. A forward method whose \(\rho\) has gauge fibres induces the leaf metric by a submersion instead, and that metric can be curved; balanced full-rank LoRA and \(B\mapsto BB^\top\) are examples. For discrete steps, the forward step lands at \(\rho(q-\eta v)\) with \(v=\Lambda D\rho_q^\top g\) and the backward step at \(\rho(q)-\eta\,D\rho_q v\); equality for all \(\eta\) is affineness along that line.

For the GaLore field, take \(L=\tfrac12\|W\|_F^2\), so \(\nabla L=W\), at \(W=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_{\min(m,n)})\) with \(\sigma_1\gt\dots\gt\sigma_{\min(m,n)}\gt0\), where \(U_r\) is spanned by the first \(r\) basis vectors. The fields \(X_E(W)=\Pi(W)E\), with \(\Pi\) the projector onto \(U_r\), span \(D_W\). First-order perturbation of \(\Pi\) gives, for \(E=e_1e_{r+1}^\top\) and \(F=e_1e_1^\top\), a bracket \([X_E,X_F]\) whose row \(r+1\) is \(\sigma_{r+1}/(\sigma_1^2-\sigma_{r+1}^2)\,e_1^\top\ne0\), so it leaves \(D\). At \(m=n=2\), \(r=1\) and \(W=\mathrm{diag}(2,1)\) this reads \([X_1,X_2]=-\tfrac13e_2e_1^\top\) for \(X_i=u(W)e_i^\top\). With a single sample, \(\nabla L=(Wx-y)x^\top\), and moving \(W\) along \(D\) keeps \(Wx-y\) in \(U_1\), so \(U_1\) is constant along \(D\) and the leaves are affine.

More: what review changed

Repaired after two reviews. The literal “only if” was false, since one integral manifold implies involutivity only along itself; the example in the statement shows it. The dynamic hypotheses are now separate. Flatness of the leaf metric is required only of immersive \(\rho\) with \(\dim Q=k\), since gauge-fibred methods escape it: balanced full-rank LoRA induces a curved leaf metric. An earlier claim that full-batch rank-one GaLore is integrable is withdrawn; the diagonal example in the proof refutes it at every rank.

More: in what sense “dual”
Analogy (the lens duality). “Tangent side” and “cotangent side” are exact words here. A forward method acts on tangent vectors through \(D\rho\), and a backward method acts on cotangent vectors through \(c\). Calling GaLore and one-sided LoRA dual is exact only at degree one, and only for optimizers that do not read the parameter tensor. Beyond that range the word organises the zoo and proves nothing.

What the backward pass must remember

Fine-tuning memory is weights, optimizer state, and activations stored for the backward pass. Shrinking \(Q\) shrinks the second. As for the third, a linear box’s reverse derivative \(W^\top y\) ignores its input, and cotangents are needed only downstream of something trainable, so only a cone through the network costs activation memory.

Observation IV.4 (backward reach and the backprop cone). Upper bound Assume reverse mode in which each box keeps its own residual, with no checkpointing across boxes, and take boxes at the granularity where adapters are separate boxes (for LoRA-FA, \(A_0\) and \(B\) separately). A VJP residual is an input, an output, a sufficient statistic such as a ReLU mask, or enough to recompute one of these within the box. Then it suffices to keep a VJP residual for
  • each trainable box whose parameter-VJP depends on its input: linear and multiplicative boxes such as \(W\), \(A\), \(B\) and (IA)³ scales; additive boxes such as biases and prompts need nothing;
  • each nonlinear box on a directed path from a trainable box to the loss.

Deterministic frozen linear boxes, and all boxes off this cone, need nothing; dropout on the cone needs its mask or random state. Alternatively, \(k\) forward-mode JVPs give the \(k\) coordinates of \(P^\top\nabla L\) exactly, with no reverse pass, \(O(1)\)-layer activation memory, and time a small constant multiple of \(k\) forward passes. That route is time-competitive with one VJP only for very small \(k\), and it needs \(P\) in advance, so it cannot produce GaLore’s SVD refresh, where \(P\) depends on \(\nabla L\), more cheaply than a backward pass.

(sketch; expand)

Cotangents are needed only on the downstream cone of the trainable boxes, so everything else can be pruned. A frozen linear box has \(\mathsf R[f](x,y)=W^\top y\), which does not depend on \(x\), whereas a nonlinear box’s reverse derivative depends on its input through some residual. A trainable linear box \(y=Wx\) has parameter-VJP \(\bar y\,x^\top\), and a multiplicative box \(y=l\odot x\) has \(\bar y\odot x\) summed over tokens, so both read \(x\). A bias’s parameter cotangent is its incoming cotangent. A dropout box’s VJP applies the same rescaled mask to \(\bar y\).

The \(i\)-th coordinate of \(P^\top\nabla L\) is the directional derivative \(DL(\theta)[p_i]\), which forward mode computes alongside a forward pass, keeping only the current layer’s values and tangents. The memory and time costs of forward mode are the standard ones of automatic differentiation and are not re-derived here.

∎

The bound reads off the activation memory of the zoo.

  • LST reads the backbone through trainable down-projection ladders, so the backbone’s backward pass never runs.
  • LoRA-FA stores \(A_0x\) (\(r\) numbers per token) instead of \(x\) (\(n\) numbers), plus the dropout mask unless lora_dropout=0, HF’s default.
  • Top-layer adaptation truncates the cone, as long as no checkpointing sends cotangents further down (see the caveat).
  • LISA trains the embedding and LM head at every step, so its cone is the whole network. Its unsampled layers’ frozen linear boxes drop their input residuals, so it keeps fewer activations than full fine-tuning or LoRA, though most of its saving is in gradients and optimizer state.
  • Prompt tuning has a \(Q\) as small as VeRA’s, FourierFT’s or (IA)³’s but the maximal cone, the whole network, and its prompt tokens enlarge every stored activation.
  • MeZO is the case \(k=1\), with a central difference in place of the JVP and no reverse pass.
CaveatWith reentrant gradient checkpointing, HF PEFT makes the embedding outputs require gradients (enable_input_require_grads), so cotangents run down to the embeddings and top-layer adaptation no longer truncates the cone.

More: fine print on the readings

LoRA-FA’s saving is real only where no neighbouring nonlinear box already keeps \(x\). MeZO’s estimate is unbiased for the gradient of the Gaussian-smoothed loss, and for a loss with Lipschitz Hessian its bias against \(\nabla L\) is \(O(\varepsilon^2)\). Prompt tuning shares the maximal cone with every method that trains something in the first layer. A LISA variant that froze the embedding would truncate the cone at its lowest sampled layer. GaLore, its random-projector variant and LISA all take the reverse route. Exact unbiasedness, \(\mathbb E[(\nabla L\cdot z)z]=\nabla L\), holds for the forward gradient computed with an exact JVP rather than MeZO’s finite difference.

Repaired after review. The observation is now an upper bound with an explicit notion of residual. Additive trainable boxes need nothing, dropout needs its mask, the JVP route is competitive in time only for very small \(k\), and MeZO’s central difference is biased at order \(\varepsilon^2\). LISA, which trains its embedding, keeps the whole cone while still dropping the residuals of its frozen linear boxes.

So every cotangent method meeting the hypotheses of Prop IV.2 is, period by period, a linear forward method on a moving base. What it reaches, and what a quantiser does to the base, are the subject of Exposé V.
